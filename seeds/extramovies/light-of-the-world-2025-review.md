Yes, Light of the World is worth watching, if your household can sit still for a sermon told in pictures. Tom Bancroft and John J. Schafer's animated telling of the Gospel, seen through the eyes of the young apostle John, is handsome, well made and completely uninterested in subtlety. The animation is the best in the frame. Rotten Tomatoes has it at 80 percent from 15 reviews. Our rating is 6.5 out of 10.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** Watch it with the family. An 84-minute hand-drawn animated film about the life of Jesus, with Ian Hanlin as Jesus and Michael Benyaer as Nicodemus, told from the point of view of John. Rotten Tomatoes has it at 80 percent from 15 critic reviews and a 97 percent Popcornmeter. You need no plot summary before pressing play.

## What Light of the World is about

The film opens in the Garden of Eden and jumps to Galilee in 30 AD, where a boy named John lives with his mother Salome and his father Zebedee, a fisherman too busy with nets and taxes to wait for the Messiah his wife keeps promising. John is sent to the market to sell fish for the tax collector Matthew, comes home with a single coin, and ends up in front of a carpenter nobody has heard of.

That carpenter is Jesus, and the film then walks through the Gospel with John as the witness: the Baptizer in the Jordan, the wedding at Cana where water becomes wine, the call of the fishermen, the healing of the paralytic and of Mary Magdalene, the feeding of the five thousand, the walk on the water and Peter's sinking, the entry into Jerusalem, the Last Supper, Gethsemane, the arrest, the trial, the cross.

Michael Benyaer voices Nicodemus, one of the council members who stays out of the plot to get rid of Jesus and turns up later to help bury him, and the film gets its clearest-eyed piece of casting out of it.

## Cast and performances

Ian Hanlin carries Jesus with an actor's ear for material that usually falls flat, keeping the divine slightly off balance so that the film never turns smug. Benjamin Jacobson is the John of the title, a child who asks the obvious questions, which is exactly why the film can work as a retelling. David A. Kaye plays Judas Iscariot with none of the usual theatrical warning signs, and Sam Darkoh's Peter is the only apostle given a genuine arc.

The voices are the argument for the film's restraint. Nobody here is doing a Bible voice, and that is why the scenes in the upper room feel like a room rather than a lesson.

## What works

The animation. Richard Smith at The Christian Film Review wrote that "The animation is superb, the 2D feels somewhat fresh, nostalgic and inviting, it stands out from the crowd of 3D animated films". Alan French at Sunshine State Cineplex said the same thing in fewer words: "The gorgeous animation alone is worth diving into this version, even if the story is fairly traditional." Hand-drawn backgrounds, warm light on the faces, and a fish market that feels like a place where people work.

The point of view. Joseph Pronechen at the National Catholic Register named the film's method: "One major key to its success is how the story is told through the eyes of the youngster John, who is befriended by Jesus before becoming his disciple." It is the difference between a film about Jesus and a film about a boy who met him.

## What doesn't

The bluntness. Luke Y. Thompson at Mortal Cinema wrote that the film "isn't much of a movie" but "a biblical tract for kids that makes sure characters always say their names out loud and repeat key scriptural points", and that is a fair description of a film that has decided not to hide. Paul Salfen at AMFM Magazine gave it nine out of ten and disagreed, writing that the film "does the story justice with top-tier animation with its heart in the right place". Both readings are fair. The film picks one and stays there.

The catechism. The film explains every miracle twice, once in action and once in a line of dialogue that no one in a village of the first century would have needed. Carla Hay at Culture Mix was kind about it and still described the film accurately: "an enjoyable 2-D animated film that gives modern dialogue to the Christian biblical story about Jesus Christ and his disciples, from the perspective of youngest apostle John."

The framing. Brett McCracken at The Gospel Coalition wrote that if you have kids, "take them to see The Light of the World", which is the review as advice rather than as criticism, and the film gives the audience no room to argue with the people who wrote it.

## Verdict

Is Light of the World worth watching? Yes, for the craft and for the children, and with the tolerance a film this literal asks for. The animation is genuinely fine, the cast reads well, and the story it tells is the oldest one there is. Paul Salfen's nine out of ten is the ceiling for anyone who loves it, and Luke Y. Thompson's objection is the floor for anyone who does not. Six and a half out of 10 is where the film lands when you average the room.
