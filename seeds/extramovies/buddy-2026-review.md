Yes, Buddy is worth watching, and it is worth watching after midnight with the lights off, because Casper Kelly's horror comedy takes the one thing that always felt wrong about children's television and turns it into a body count. Keegan-Michael Key plays a singing orange unicorn who becomes a tyrant, the child actors around him are better than the concept deserves, and the jokes stop working about halfway through. Critics split on it. Rotten Tomatoes has it at 88 percent from 138 critic reviews and Metacritic at 67 from 22, both checked on October 22, 2026.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** Watch it if you grew up on kids' shows and you want them back in a worse shape. ## What Buddy is about

It's Buddy! is a 1990s television programme for children, and the film plays the show in full before the story turns. The sets are built, the aspect ratio is right, the video has the grain of a tape, and the orange unicorn host walks around a bright studio telling everyone what to do. The children sing the theme together, to the tune of Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star. Tomris Laffly at RogerEbert.com wrote out the lyric and the melody in the same breath: "the show's jingle with the lyrics 'You love Buddy, yes you do!' sang to the tune of 'Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star,' would like to remind you. (Yes, this is as hilariously creepy as it sounds.)"

The cast is the show's cast. Delaney Quinn plays Freddy, Caleb Williams plays Wade, Luke Speakman plays Josh, Madison Skyy Polan plays Hannah and Tristan Borders plays Oliver. Patton Oswalt voices Strappy, Jimmica Collins voices Betty Bunny and the Scared Flower, and Eric Bauza voices Mr. Mailbox, Worry Well and Couchy. Buddy himself is voiced by Keegan-Michael Key, who shares the suit duties with Sergey Zhuravsky.

The show runs until one child stops cooperating. Josh does not feel like joining in, and that is the whole engine of the picture: a friendly programme with an authoritarian at the centre, and the first crack. Laffly's word for the result was that Buddy is "bloody annoying, even tyrannical."

Elsewhere, in the real world, Ben (Topher Grace) and Grace (Cristin Milioti) are a couple who are missing a child they cannot remember.

## Cast and performances

Key is doing two jobs at once, and the suit is half of it. The trick is that the performance has to keep selling the show within the show, cheerful and shallow, right up to the moment it does not.

The children are the best thing in the film, and they have the hardest job. Laffly's assessment was that "the young cast act at an impossible intersection of a TV show that demands artificial emotions and real-life fears such as death, abandonment, and loneliness." That is the engine of the movie, and they carry it.

Cristin Milioti plays Grace, the mother in the real world who starts to suspect the gap in her family is not a memory problem, and she plays it with the tight, cheerful register the film needs. Tim Stevens at The Spool noted the effect: "For the world it creates and a Milioti who earnestly commits, it's worth a watch."

Topher Grace plays Ben, who has no idea what his wife is starting to remember.

## What works

The idea, and the refusal to explain it. Monica Castillo at The A.V. Club noted that the film is "more than just a pastiche, it's its own weirdo film built from TV parts, a horror movie about childhood that's meant for adults who can look back at their own half-forgotten shows, laugh, and wonder why they liked these oddly endearing, sometimes alarming, programs." That is exactly the register the picture runs in.

The production design, which is rigorously cheap on purpose: a period aspect ratio, a studio built out of painted flats, puppet work for the mascots, and costume design that copies the shows it is parodying.

The midnight-movie instinct. Christian Zilko at IndieWire wrote that "Simply put, Buddy is everything you could want from a midnight movie. It gets harder and harder to find something that feels fresh enough to be truly shocking and executed competently enough to transcend its gimmicks, and we should all celebrate when we find one."

And the gore, which is funny. Tim Grierson at Screen Daily pointed out that the kill scenes are staged for laughs rather than for fear, and that Kelly finds fresh ways to exploit the idea anyway.

## What doesn't

The plot. Erik Piepenburg at The New York Times called it "Casper Kelly's chilling and funny but muddled genre roller coaster", and the paper's own summary line was blunter: "Barney but Satan: That's the macabre world Casper Kelly posits in his horror comedy about an adorable but monstrous children's television unicorn." Carla Hay at Culture Mix found the same wobble from the other side: "The offbeat horror comedy Buddy has a jumbled plot where the jokes don't always work."

The pacing. Tom Jorgensen at IGN gave the film 80 and credited Kelly with overcoming "some significant pacing hitches", which is a polite way of saying the middle sags before the picture finds its second gear.

The comedy. The more blood there is, the less the gags land, and Kelly keeps the blood coming.

## Verdict

Is Buddy worth watching? Yes, and put it on after midnight, with the sound on, because the puppetry and the gore are doing a lot of work that the plot is not. Casper Kelly took a relentlessly cheerful children's programme and turned it into a hostage situation, and the first hour is funny and genuinely unsettling. The second hour is where the jokes start to fail and the blood starts to substitute for them.

The numbers split cleanly. Rotten Tomatoes says 88 percent, Metacritic says 67 and TMDB says 7.1, all of them above our verdict, while Letterboxd says 3.12, which is 6.24 out of 10 from more than 300,000 raters, just below it. Critics are right that this is a strange and funny picture. Viewers are right that the plot is a jumble. Both of those are the same movie.

## Spoiler warning: the ending of Buddy

Josh is the first to die, and it happens off screen, in a way the film never shows you. What triggers it is the one child who says he does not want to take part, and what turns Buddy into a killer is hearing that the children do not like him. After that the rampage is public and it does not stop. Wade dies next, stabbed with Buddy's horn while Freddy tries to reach him. Hannah is dragged off screen. Oliver is swallowed whole.

The children's escape is the "Wizard of Oz" journey the film set up, an escape across an enchanted forest where they have to fend for themselves, and it nearly works because of the one adult who is still on their side. Grace, Freddy's mother, gives them the time they need and pays for it with her life.

The mascot characters are not safe either. Mailman Miles, Mailman Justin and Nurse Nancy are killed as Buddy comes further apart, Willie and George die in the woods, and Charlie the Train and Betty Bunny have their heads torn off. Strappy and Mr. Mailbox survive Buddy, but they arrive in the real world as ordinary objects.

Freddy is the only child who gets out. She comes through into a television station full of sets, and other sets, and children wired into them, and she understands that this is how it is done: something like Buddy pulls in new cast mates and erases them from where they were. The similar costumes in the studio suggest there are other shows doing the same thing, and Buddy is not the one who started it. The shining city on the horizon turns out to be a model.

She lands in a different country with no way to call home. Ben and Grace are never reunited, and Grace's death is never explained to him. Kelly answers nothing and tells you on purpose, and the last thing the film does is show you a child walking away from a studio with the credits rolling.
