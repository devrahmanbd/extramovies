Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets is the most faithful Harry Potter film and the least edited one, and at 161 minutes those are the same problem. Chris Columbus returned for his second and final turn behind the camera, kept the second book nearly whole, and delivered the series' longest cut. The production design doubles the world, Kenneth Branagh's Gilderoy Lockhart is the franchise's sharpest comic performance, and the finale stages the saga's first true monster fight. The middle hour waits for its clues. Rotten Tomatoes carries the film at 82 percent from 234 critics, Metacritic scores it 63 from 35, and TMDB holds 7.7 from 24,392 votes, all checked November 12, 2026. Produced for $100 million, the film grossed $876,688,482 worldwide, per TMDB.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** The book-est of the eight films, and the one that most needs a pair of scissors. Dobby is a properly unsettling early warning system, Branagh photobombs every scene with delicious self-regard, and the basilisk finale pays the mystery off in full. Between the set pieces the cut lumbers: the detective story stalls in repeated classroom beats, and the book's darkness arrives in one dose at the end. Watch it for the world and for Lockhart. It holds up, slowly.

## What Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets is about

An elf crashes Harry's birthday with a warning: terrible things are coming to Hogwarts, and Harry should not go back. He goes back anyway, by flying car, and the car misjudges a hedge and takes a beating from the Whomping Willow. The warning starts scoring points at school. A wall of written blood announces that the Chamber of Secrets has been opened. Students are found petrified, stiff as statues, and nobody knows what is doing it. Harry alone hears a voice sliding through the castle's pipes, and the trail runs through a small black diary that belonged to a boy named Tom Riddle, dead for half a century.

Steve Kloves's screenplay keeps the mystery frame from the novel and lets the school absorb the blows. The new faces carry it: Lockhart, a Defense Against the Dark Arts teacher whose book line outruns his ability, and Dobby, the house-elf whose loyalty to Harry keeps coming out as self-harm.

## The craft: Stuart Craig's bigger world

Stuart Craig's production design does the film's real work. The Burrow arrives as a stack of mismatched rooms with a clock that tracks family members instead of hours. Knockturn Alley runs its shrunken-head shop under green, gutted light, and the Chamber entrance waits behind a row of bathroom sinks, the kind of joke the franchise would only be allowed once. Roger Pratt photographs it warm indoors and cold out, and John Williams returned for his second Potter score, per TMDB's credits.

Dobby is the film's riskiest technical bet. The elf runs on 2002-era computer graphics, and the seams show in a few closeups, but the design keeps an actor's timing: he punishes himself on cue, and his last scene, where Harry tricks Lucius Malfoy into freeing him with a sock, is a genuine payoff.

Branagh is the film's comic engine. Columbus shoots Lockhart like a parody of a Hollywood premiere: signed portraits and violet robes, and a smile that never stops working. Michael Sragow's Baltimore Sun review called Branagh "one bright light in Chamber of Secrets," and its other half, "drags on and on," is also on the record.

## What works

The duelling club is the series' best early staging of magic as combat: Lockhart's theatrics, Draco's serpent, and Harry's answer in a language that makes the whole hall read the wrong lesson. The diary flashback compresses 1943 into one visit, with a young Dumbledore calling on Hagrid, and the film's past and present click together in under three minutes.

The finale is the payoff the mystery owes. The basilisk is the biggest creature the series had built by 2002, and Columbus shoots it like a haunted-house set piece: petrified stares, flooded floor, a sword that arrives on time. It earns the scene.

## What doesn't

At 161 minutes the film runs ten minutes beyond Goblet of Fire's 157, and the extra length goes to repetition instead of revelation. The detective plot advances in stationary steps: a petrification, a clue, a classroom, another petrification. Variety's Todd McCarthy caught the problem exactly, calling the film "a bit overlong and unmodulated in pacing." Peter Rainer, in New York Magazine, diagnosed "a bit of sequelitis," and the Washington Post's Stephen Hunter went further with "Big, dull and empty."

The film's own 35-critic distribution on Metacritic tells the same story: 23 positive, 11 mixed, 1 negative, for a 63 Metascore that is the lowest of the eight films. Rotten Tomatoes' critics consensus calls the film "more enchanting for younger audiences" than its predecessor, which is a polite way of saying the first hour plays young. The other cost is tonal: the book's dread builds in chapters, and the film holds it out of sight until the last act.

## Chamber of Secrets ending explained

Spoiler-free summary first. Harry wins the mystery, the school recovers, and the person who suffered most gets an ovation at the final assembly. How it works is the film's real plot.

Riddle's diary is the engine. Lucius Malfoy slips it into Ginny Weasley's cauldron at Flourish and Blotts, and the memory preserved inside it feeds on her and uses her to reopen the Chamber and write the warnings. Those the basilisk petrified survived only because they saw it through something: a mirror, a camera, water, a ghost. Moaning Myrtle, who died in the Chamber's first opening fifty years earlier, haunts the bathroom that hides the entrance, and her sink is the way down.

In the Chamber, Riddle shows himself as the preserved memory of the boy who framed Hagrid for the first attacks. Ron's broken wand backfires on Lockhart, who came to erase the boys' memories and loses his own. The Sorting Hat delivers the Sword of Gryffindor, Fawkes the phoenix claws the basilisk blind and carries the sword to Harry, and Harry kills the monster with a fang through the roof of its mouth. Stabbing the diary with a second fang kills the memory and revives Ginny. The sock handoff frees Dobby from the Malfoys, and Hagrid walks back into the great hall to an ovation.

## The verdict

Is Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets worth watching? Yes, at the right speed. The case for the film is concrete: Craig's world doubles in size, Branagh gives the franchise its sharpest satire of celebrity, and the basilisk finale is a properly staged monster fight the mystery earns. The case against is just as concrete: the longest cut in the series spends its extra minutes repeating classroom beats, the petrification plot advances in stationary steps, and the computer graphics age unevenly around Dobby. The 82 percent Tomatometer, the 63 Metascore from 35 critics, and the 80 percent Popcornmeter from 250,000+ ratings are the receipts, checked November 12, 2026. It is the entry you rewatch when you want the full book, and the one you skip when you want the saga to move.
