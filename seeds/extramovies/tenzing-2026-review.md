## What Tenzing is about

The film opens with Tenzing as a boy staring across the valley at Mount Everest, which he calls by its Tibetan name, Chomolungma, a name that translates as holy mother. That translation is the argument of the film: the British team talks about taking the peak, while Tenzing treats it as ground he is allowed to stand on, and the movie keeps those two views of the same mountain in play from the first scene. Early on he meets the British climber George Mallory, played by Johnny Flynn, a veteran of the first three Everest expeditions in the 1920s. The boy grows up in the shadow of a peak nobody had yet reached, and by the 1953 British expedition, eight previous attempts had failed, by Variety's count.

That expedition is run by Colonel John Hunt, who insists the whole mission be managed as a military operation. The film sets his logic against his blind spot: Hunt's system puts the Sherpa at the bottom of the order even though the team cannot climb without them. Tenzing works his way onto the climbing party rather than merely serving it, helped by his wife Dawa and by the expedition secretary Jill Henderson, played by Caitríona Balfe. Tom Hiddleston plays Edmund Hillary, the New Zealander who becomes Tenzing's climbing partner.

Variety's Owen Gleiberman read the politics straight: a British team staking out the last of its empire, with Sherpa officially listed as guides who knew the mountain far better than the officers did. The history is documented; the dialogue and intimate scenes are dramatization.

## How the climb was filmed

Peedom spent years making mountain documentaries, including 2015's Sherpa, which put her in touch with Tenzing's children. His eldest son, Norbu Tenzing, is an executive producer on the feature. Principal photography started in Nepal in May 2025 on location in Kathmandu and the Everest region, and wrapped in New Zealand that August, with Stefan Duscio behind the camera. The mountain work reads as physically real, the payoff of shooting on location instead of on a set.

The performances carry the human argument. Dafoe plays Hunt as competent and blinkered rather than villainous, Hiddleston keeps Hillary contained, and Phuntsok gives Tenzing a steadiness that never tips into passivity. Critics split on how far the film pushes its own themes: Rotten Tomatoes' consensus calls it an immersive, tasteful tribute to a pioneer history overlooked, while The Upcoming's 3 of 5 says the script only hints at the colonisation and prejudice sitting underneath it, and Metacritic's 67, well below the 98% Tomatometer, is that reservation in numbers.

Tenzing is not a documentary record of the ascent; the difference matters most in the final act, where the film makes one deliberate invention about how the summit was shot.

## Tenzing ending explained

![Tenzing review poster: Genden Phuntsok as Tenzing Norgay with Mount Everest behind him](https://image.tmdb.org/t/p/w500/eI0QW02PYqhnmIvLRdn9vrfVVP7.jpg)

The last stretch begins with a failed attempt. Two other members of the team, Tom Bourdillon and Charles Evans, are chosen for the summit push first, and complications end that plan, which leaves Hillary and Tenzing as the pair who go. On the way up the film stages two near disasters: Hillary drops into a crevice and Tenzing holds the rope until he is hauled out, and later Tenzing, hypoxic and disoriented, is saved when Hillary gets his oxygen flowing again.

Then comes the choice the whole film has been building toward. The camera watches from a distance, from behind, as the two men take steps in turn, so the frame never tells you who technically arrived first. ScreenRant's ending breakdown notes the exchange right before the final steps: each man says "After you," and they walk up together. Peedom told Yahoo Entertainment why she shot it that way: who got there first is the question everyone asks, and to Tenzing and Hillary the question made no sense, because neither could have done it without the other.

That is the ending answer, thematic rather than historical: the debate over who reached the summit first has run for decades, and the film declines to pick a side. The closing image follows the same logic: Tenzing stands slightly apart from the celebrating group after the return to camp, looks back up at the mountain, and lets out a long, contented breath before the cut to black. The credits then run over real footage of the lifelong friendship between the two men. History supplies the summit; the film supplies the meaning it held for the man history sidelined.

## What viewers say

The headline scores hide a gap: critics were harder on Tenzing than fans were. The IMDb and TMDB review pages, read on October 29, 2026, sort the praise into four clusters. Genden Phuntsok is the one almost nobody argues with, a lead who plays Tenzing with patience instead of swagger. The Chomolungma framing draws steady approval from viewers who want the peak treated as sacred ground for the people who live under it rather than as a trophy. The balance of the story is the third cluster: viewers keep making the point that the film takes Tenzing's half of the history without taking anything from Edmund Hillary, which is the hard part of any shared achievement. The multilingual script is the fourth, defended by viewers who say the mix of Sherpa, Nepali, Tibetan and English is why the climb feels lived in rather than staged.

The complaints group just as tightly. The screenplay is called sanitized and low on friction, with flashbacks that repeat more than they add. Secondary parts are said to be thin: Caitríona Balfe has little to do as Jill Henderson, the family at home gets little room, and Dawa drops out of the middle of the film. Several reviewers think the British expedition members take more screen time than the story needs, and a smaller, harsher note calls the storm and oxygen work visibly cheap. One observation of our own: the film never presses the distance between 1953 and today's commercially supported climbs, where logistics are bought rather than improvised.

## The verdict

Tenzing does the thing biopics usually skip. It treats the 1953 ascent as a story about who gets to be the protagonist of their own achievement, and it lands the argument in the last shot rather than in a speech. The reframing works because it never turns into a correction: Tenzing takes the center of the story while Hillary stays a partner rather than a rival, and the mountain stays sacred ground to one man and a target to the other. Genden Phuntsok is the reason, and Peedom's decision to shoot the mountain as a place rather than an opponent gives the film its weight. The reservation is real too: the sharpest material, the empire and the hierarchy, stays in the background, which is why Metacritic sits at 67 while the Tomatometer is at 98%.
