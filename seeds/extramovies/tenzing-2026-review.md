# Tenzing Review: Ending, Release Date, Where to Watch

The Tenzing review starts with the scoreboard, checked October 29, 2026: 98% on Rotten Tomatoes from 63 reviews, 67 on Metacritic from 18 critics, 7.5/10 on IMDb, and 8.3/10 on TMDB from 208 votes. Jennifer Peedom's Everest drama premiered at Telluride on September 4, played a limited theatrical run in the United States and Australia on October 9, and reached Apple TV+ worldwide on October 16. This Tenzing review gives the verdict first, then the climb, the ending question the director refused to settle, and exactly where to watch it.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** A ground-level 1953 Everest drama that hands Tenzing Norgay the headline history gave to everyone around him. Genden Phuntsok anchors it, Willem Dafoe's Colonel John Hunt supplies the hierarchy politics, and the summit sequence plays as partnership instead of contest. TMDB users rate it 8.3. ## What Tenzing is about

The film opens with Tenzing as a boy staring across the valley at Mount Everest, which he calls by its Tibetan name, Chomolungma. Early on he meets the British climber George Mallory, played by Johnny Flynn in a small but pointed appearance, because Mallory was part of the first three Everest expeditions in the 1920s. The boy grows up in the shadow of a peak nobody had yet reached, and by the time the story reaches the 1953 British expedition, eight previous attempts had already failed, by Variety's count.

That expedition is run by Colonel John Hunt, who insists the whole mission be managed as a military operation. The film sets his logic against his blind spot: Hunt's system puts the Sherpa at the bottom of the order even though the team cannot climb without them. Tenzing works his way onto the climbing party rather than merely serving it, helped by his wife Dawa and by the expedition secretary Jill Henderson, played by Caitríona Balfe. Tom Hiddleston plays Edmund Hillary, the New Zealander who becomes Tenzing's climbing partner.

Variety's Owen Gleiberman read the politics straight: a British team staking out the last of its empire, with Sherpa officially listed as guides who knew the mountain far better than the officers did. The history is documented, but the dialogue and the intimate scenes are dramatization, and the film should be watched on those terms.

## How the climb was filmed

Peedom spent years making mountain documentaries, including 2015's Sherpa, and this is her first large-scale dramatic staging of the material. Principal photography started in Nepal in May 2025 on location in Kathmandu and the Everest region, and wrapped in New Zealand that August, with Stefan Duscio behind the camera. The mountain work reads as physically real, which is the practical payoff of that decision: audiences notice the difference between bodies on rock and bodies in front of a screen.

The performances carry the human argument. Dafoe plays Hunt as competent and blinkered rather than villainous, Hiddleston keeps Hillary contained, and Phuntsok gives Tenzing a steadiness that never tips into passivity. Critics split on how far the film pushes its own themes. Rotten Tomatoes' consensus calls it an immersive, tastful tribute to a pioneer history overlooked, while The Upcoming's 3 of 5 review argues the script only hints at the colonisation and prejudice sitting underneath it. Metacritic's 67, well below the 98% Tomatometer, is the numeric version of that same reservation.

It is worth naming what Tenzing is not: a documentary record of the ascent. It is a dramatization of a documented 1953 event, and the difference matters most in the final act, where the film makes its one deliberate invention about how the summit was shot.

## Tenzing ending explained

![Tenzing review poster: Genden Phuntsok as Tenzing Norgay with Mount Everest behind him](https://image.tmdb.org/t/p/w500/eI0QW02PYqhnmIvLRdn9vrfVVP7.jpg)

The last stretch begins with a failed attempt. Two other members of the team, Tom Bourdillon and Charles Evans, are chosen for the summit push first, and complications end that plan, which leaves Hillary and Tenzing as the pair who go. On the way up the film stages two near disasters: Hillary drops into a crevice and Tenzing holds the rope until he is hauled out, and later Tenzing, hypoxic and disoriented, is saved when Hillary gets his oxygen flowing again. The wife he lost earlier in the film also appears to him in a vision.

Then comes the choice the whole film has been building toward. The camera watches from a distance, from behind, as the two men take steps in turn, so the frame never tells you who technically arrived first. ScreenRant's ending breakdown notes the exchange right before the final steps: each man says "After you," and they walk up together. Director Jennifer Peedom told Yahoo Entertainment why she shot it that way: who got to the top first is the question everyone asks, and to Tenzing and Hillary the question made no sense, because neither could have done it without the other.

That is the answer to the ending question, and it is a thematic answer rather than a historical one. The debate over which man reached the summit first has run for decades, and the film declines to pick a side. The closing image follows the same logic: Tenzing stands slightly apart from the celebrating group after the return to camp, looks back up at the mountain, and lets out a long, contented breath before the cut to black. The credits then run over real footage of the lifelong friendship between the two men. History supplies the summit; the film supplies the meaning it held for the man history sidelined.

## The verdict

Tenzing does the thing biopics usually skip. It treats the 1953 ascent as a story about who gets to be the protagonist of their own achievement, and it lands the argument in the last shot rather than in a speech. Genden Phuntsok is the reason, and Peedom's decision to shoot the mountain as a place rather than an opponent gives the film its weight. The reservation is real too: the script keeps its sharpest material, the empire and the hierarchy, in the background, which is why Metacritic sits at 67 while the Tomatometer is at 98%.
