Yes, Fire and Ice is worth watching. Ralph Bakshi's 1983 sword and sorcery feature still looks like a Frank Frazetta painting that learned to move, and the art is the part that has held up. The script is a different story. It is thin and it circles back on itself, asking you to accept a world drawn with far more care than the story sitting underneath it. Blue Underground put the picture on 4K UHD on June 30, 2026, with a Dolby Atmos track, a Bakshi commentary and a bonus soundtrack CD, so the film is easier to see today than it has been in decades.

Bakshi directed, Frazetta produced and designed the world, and Gerry Conway and Roy Thomas, who had written Conan for Marvel, scripted it. The AFI Catalog records 82 minutes, an MPAA certificate of PG, and a US release on August 19, 1983, after the January premiere at the Avoriaz Film Festival in France. The budget was $10 million.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** Watch it if you care about Frazetta's artwork or about Bakshi's nerve in shooting live action and tracing it by hand. Skip it if you need a story that carries its own weight for 82 minutes. ## What Fire and Ice is about

The story opens at the end of the last Ice Age. In the frozen north, Queen Juliana and her son Nekron rule from a citadel called Ice Peak, and they push glaciers south until humanity is retreating toward the equator. Nekron fights with magic. King Jarol holds the other side in a volcano citadel called Firekeep, and when a delegation arrives to ask for his surrender, the price is his daughter Teegra, taken to be a bride and an heir.

Teegra gets away on the road and runs into Larn, a young warrior and the only survivor of a village the glaciers took. That village is his reason for moving. A third party turns up soon after, a masked warrior called Darkwolf who has his own account to settle with the ice kingdom and says little about it.

Jarol's answer is patience. He will hold Firekeep until the glacier reaches his border, and the volcano under the citadel is the reason he can wait. The film runs on two clocks, one melting and one burning, with a princess in the middle of it.

## Cast and performances

The odd thing about this cast is that there are two of them. Bakshi shot live action and had animators trace it, so most roles have a performance model and a separate voice actor. Randy Norton plays Larn and William Ostrander voices him. Cynthia Leake plays Teegra and Maggie Roswell voices her. Sean Hannon plays Nekron and Stephen Mendel voices him. Eileen O'Neill plays Queen Juliana and Susan Tyrrell voices her. Some performers did both jobs. Steve Sandor is Darkwolf, Leo Gordon is King Jarol, and William Ostrander also plays Jarol's son Taro.

The voices are the weaker half of the double act. Tyrrell gives Juliana a cold patience, Mendel makes Nekron sound like a man enjoying his work, and Roswell gives Teegra a lightness the script rarely asks for. The acting you can see is better than the acting you can hear, and that gap is where the film feels its age.

## What works

The art. Frazetta's design sense drives the picture: the armour, the musculature, the ice palace, the volcano. The backgrounds were painted by James Gurney, later the illustrator of the Dinotopia books, and by Thomas Kinkade, and Peter Chung worked on the layouts and went on to create Aeon Flux. Everything you see is a drawing, and the drawings are the reason anyone remembers this film.

The fights. The rotoscoping gives the combat a weight the rest of the picture does not have. AFI's production notes describe a film shot twice, first as live action and then with animated characters traced over the actors, with no sets and no special lighting, only the physical movements of the actors. So a blow lands where the body actually swung, and a body goes down at the speed a body goes down. Every strike is copied from a real body, and that is why the fight scenes still work when the dialogue does not.

## What doesn't

The script. Conway and Thomas were good at Conan and less good here. The film is a rescue plot, and the rescue happens more than once: Teegra is taken, Teegra is free, Teegra is taken again, and every cycle resets the tension. Gene Siskel of the Chicago Tribune gave it two stars out of four and called it "attractive to look at, but its slow-moving, predictable story makes viewing it much like reading a comic book with pages made of lead."

The comedy. Sheila Benson of the Los Angeles Times wrote that "in spite of all the glorious washes in the background, which do indeed have the Frazetta look, Fire and Ice is as unintentionally funny a fantasy as you could hope for." Some of it plays as a joke. Some of it plays as a mistake, and the film never knows which.

The money. Wikipedia records the box office at $860,000 against that $10 million budget. AFI has Frazetta telling the November 1983 Moviegoer he would have preferred a more classic adventure story.

## What audiences say

Metacritic has the film at 45 from eight critic reviews, with a user score of 6.2 from 13 ratings. Letterboxd's member average sits at 3.12 from 21,529 ratings across 5,956 written reviews. TMDB's rating is 6.7 from 375 votes. Every figure here was checked on October 20, 2026. Rotten Tomatoes is the odd one out. Its Fire and Ice page now serves a 1994 drama about sibling ice dancers, so the 60 percent from ten critic reviews that Wikipedia's reception section records can only be read as a snapshot.

Janet Maslin of The New York Times had the sharpest line of the original release: "If you love comic books but can't bear the unnecessary bother of turning pages, Fire and Ice [...] may be for you. It would help if you were a sex-obsessed 12-year-old boy, but it isn't essential." In 2003 the Online Film Critics Society ranked it the 99th greatest animated film of all time, which is roughly where the audience has parked it. The fans who go looking for it know it well. Everyone else has never heard of it.

## Verdict

Is Fire and Ice worth watching? Yes. Bakshi's rotoscoping still reads as a dare nobody else took up, and Frazetta's art is the reason the film has outlived its own reviews. Put it on a big screen if you can find one, and know that you are watching a painting fight.

## Spoiler warning: the ending of Fire and Ice

The assault on Ice Peak comes with Jarol's dragon hawk riders in the air, and only Darkwolf gets through to Nekron. Larn, cut off from the fight, finds Teegra and gets her out while Juliana comes after them. Darkwolf takes Nekron's magic head on and kills him, and Nekron's dying spasm drives the glacier forward in one last surge. That is the moment Jarol has been waiting for. He opens the volcano, the lava runs down the mountain, and the flow takes the glacier, Ice Peak, Juliana and the subhuman army with it.

Larn and Teegra get clear of the cataclysm together. On the way they come across a wounded subhuman, Larn starts to finish it, and Teegra stops him and says the time for peace has come. They kiss. From a cliff above them, Darkwolf watches the pair, smiling, and then he is gone.
