Yes, Gandhi Talks is worth watching, with conditions. Kishor Pandurang Belekar's silent comedy asks a crowd to sit through a story with no dialogue, and for its first hour the gamble pays off. A.R. Rahman's score carries the jokes, the city and the two broken men at its centre. Then the script loads up with exposition and the film sags. Our rating is 6.5 out of 10.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** Watch it if you can give a wordless film your attention. A 133-minute silent comedy, out in Hindi, Malayalam, Telugu and Tamil, carried by Vijay Sethupathi as the unemployed Mahadev and Arvind Swamy as the builder whose empire is collapsing, with an A.R. Rahman score doing the talking. Rotten Tomatoes had 5 critic reviews and no score when we checked on October 22, 2026. You need no plot summary before pressing play.

## What Gandhi Talks is about

Mahadev (Vijay Sethupathi) has no job, an ailing mother, an electricity bill he cannot pay, and a girlfriend, Gayatri (Aditi Rao Hydari), whose parents are arranging her marriage to a man who earns thirty thousand rupees a month. His father worked for the Brihan Mumbai Corporation, so Mahadev has the right to a job there. He goes for the interview, catches a pickpocket on the way, and then fails it because he answered honestly.

The pickpocket turns up again in an air-conditioned cab with money he did not have before. Mahadev starts to think about a shortcut. He takes a job as a mourner's poser at a ceremonial home, where a child throws ice cream at him, and that is where he meets Mohan Boseman (Arvind Swamy), a builder who lost his wife, daughter and sister in a plane crash, built a skyscraper for his half-paralysed mother, watched politicians burn its floors, and has just lost his bungalow to a court that his own lawyer helped buy. Siddharth Jadhav plays Mangu, the pickpocket who becomes the film's third pole, and Boseman is planning to fake his own death and burn the house down with it.

The picture of Mahatma Gandhi on the wall is not an accident. S.R. Praveen wrote in The Hindu that "the title of the film appears as a lament for Gandhi being reduced to just a face on the currency notes (and sanitation campaigns), because the ideals that he lived by are hard to reach for."

## Cast and performances

Sethupathi plays Mahadev with his hands and his face doing all the work, and the film is at its best when it stays close to him: the bus, the chawl, the interview room, the moment he cannot put food on his mother's plate. Swamy gives Boseman a long silence of his own, the stillness of a man who has already grieved everything he had, and the two never share a frame without the class difference between them sitting in the middle of it.

Aditi Rao Hydari plays Gayatri as the person who refuses to be bought off. The Times of India's Renuka Vyavahare gave it three and a half stars and called the film "a tender portrait of humanity in a chaotic world". Her performance is the reason the ending has any shape at all.

## What works

The silence. Filmfare's Devesh Sharma made the film's central gamble the argument for the film: "Silence here is not a gimmick; it is the language. Every glance, hesitation and pause carries weight, demanding that the audience lean in rather than sit back." The Times of India's Vyavahare made the same point from the other side, writing that "crafting a silent film is one thing; making it consistently engaging is another, and Gandhi Talks succeeds on both counts."

A.R. Rahman's music, which carries the film's whole argument. Praveen noticed that it "occupies almost the entire duration of the film, often acting as a stand-in for the words that have been silenced", and the composer himself called the picture his showreel and a gift for a composer when it premiered at the 54th International Film Festival of India.

The city. The camera stays on Mumbai's streets and chawls with real affection, and the film earns its name without a word of dialogue.

## What doesn't

The exposition. Once Mahadev's scheme is explained, it is explained again, and then a third time by characters who had no reason to know it. Nandini Ramnath at Scroll.in named the problem exactly: "Gandhi Talks has too much character information and plot exposition for a movie that viewers are supposed to follow despite the absence of spoken lines."

The back half. The Indian Express's Shubhra Gupta, who gave the film two stars, put it more bluntly: "The better bits are in the first half; post-interval, all is a confused slump, weighed down by an inordinately long passage with the rich guy, poor fellow and the grinning thief skulking about without any discernible purpose." The second half drags, and it feels longer.

The craft. Vinamra Mathur at Firstpost allowed that the film is "silly, satirical, satisfying, if not necessarily spectacular in its craft", and that is the right summary. Belekar had the idea for 23 years before shooting it, and some of that patience shows in the framing while the rest shows up as slack.

## Verdict

Is Gandhi Talks worth watching? Yes, for the first hour, for Rahman's score, and for a cast that plays a whole argument without saying a word of it. Shubhra Gupta's two stars are for a film that loses its nerve after the interval, and she is not wrong. Her two stars land on the script. The score covers what the second half gives up, and that is where our extra half point comes from. Six and a half out of 10.

## Spoiler warning: the ending of Gandhi Talks

Mahadev does break into Boseman's bungalow and takes money. Gayatri catches him with it in his hands and refuses to let him keep it, and that is the moment the film turns: he goes back to the house and returns the cash. Boseman has fuses set through the rooms and a body from the morgue waiting in the bathtub, ready for the fire to make the death official. He sees what is in Mahadev's face, recognises the same corruption that has emptied his own life, and lets him go. The bungalow burns behind them as they walk away in opposite directions.

Mahadev stops chasing money. In a few months the Brihan Mumbai Corporation hires him, and he can finally pay for his mother's medicine, keep the lights on and give Gayatri a future that is not a rumour. They stay together. The last thing the film shows is the picture of Gandhi on the wall, and the idea that honesty, not money, decides how a life goes.
