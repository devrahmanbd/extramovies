Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban is the film where the Harry Potter series acquires weather and dread under a director with a distinct hand, and it is still the critics' favorite of the eight. Alfonso Cuarón took the third book the year Richard Harris died, and the franchise's tonal pivot happened in one production: Michael Gambon steps in as Dumbledore, Gary Oldman arrives as the escaped prisoner Sirius Black, and the castle's colors drop from gilt into grey and rain. The receipts match the reputation. Rotten Tomatoes carries the film at 91 percent from 260 critics, Metacritic scores it 82 from 40, and TMDB holds 8.0 from 23,966 votes, all checked November 12, 2026. Produced for $130 million, the film grossed $789,804,554 worldwide, per TMDB. The plot flits in its middle stretch. The atmosphere carries everything around it.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** The saga's tonal hinge and its best-photographed film. Oldman's wire-tight Sirius and Thewlis's gentle, damaged Lupin give the series its first adult tragedy. The dementors are properly frightening, and the time-turner finale double-exposes one afternoon into the franchise's best pure sequence. The middle sags into school-day straggle and drops the book's backstory. Watch it for the atmosphere, the two new actors, and the lake scene. It holds up beautifully.

## What Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban is about

Harry's summer ends with Aunt Marge inflated into a parade balloon, and his third year starts with a manhunt: Sirius Black has escaped the wizard prison Azkaban, and the Ministry believes he betrayed Harry's parents. The dementors board the Hogwarts Express to search the train, and the temperature of the whole series drops with them. Around the alert, school carries on: a new Defense teacher, Remus Lupin, whose boggart lesson is the franchise's best classroom; Hagrid's first Care of Magical Creatures class on the half-horse, half-eagle Buckbeak; and a rat, a dog, and a magic map that keep crossing paths around a boy hearing his mother scream.

## The craft: dementors, fog, and a choir from Macbeth

The dementors are the film's design centerpiece, and Cuarón's team built them out of subtraction: they glide instead of walk, and breath fogs in the train corridor before the thing itself appears. Michael Seresin's photography, per TMDB's credits, gives the series its first wintry palette, and Stuart Craig's sets rebuild the grounds around the clock tower and a new Hogsmeade village. The whomping willow moves, and the castle stops feeling like a theme park.

The score is John Williams's third and final for the series, and Wikipedia's soundtrack record ties the departure to the director's brief: the album introduces "A Window to the Past," "Buckbeak's Flight," and "Double Trouble," a choir piece whose lyrics are the witches' lines from Macbeth. The film's tagline, "Something wicked this way comes," comes from the same play.

The biggest technical bet is Buckbeak, and it landed: feathers, breath, and pride all read on screen.

## What works

Oldman plays Sirius as a man twelve years of dementors stripped of everything except the reason he went in, and Thewlis's Lupin is the series' best teacher: gentle, precise, and hiding something the film shows in his hands before it says it out loud. The boggart lesson is the franchise's best classroom scene, Snape in a dress and a spider on roller skates included, and Hermione's punch to Malfoy's face remains the most cheered cut in the series. The Shrieking Shack confrontation is the best single scene the franchise has staged: three kids, two fugitives, and a full moon waiting outside. The reveal rewrites everything that came before it, and the film plays fair with every clue.

The time-turner finale is the franchise's best pure filmmaking, and Cuarón re-photographs one afternoon from the other side: the pebbles through Hagrid's window, the axe on a pumpkin, the Patronus nobody on screen can explain yet. The silver stag resolves out of fog.

## What doesn't

The middle of the film is its own worst enemy. Ann Hornaday's Washington Post dissent calls it "one long sit, made all the more so by a turgid story, a dour visual palette and uninspiring action," and while the action charge is unfair to the dementor sequences, the sit is real: the school-day connective tissue wanders. The film's 141 minutes run 20 under Chamber of Secrets while somehow feeling slower in the middle.

The book's backstory is the bigger casualty. The film never explains that Moony, Wormtail, Padfoot, and Prongs, the four names signed on the Marauder's Map, are Lupin, Pettigrew, Sirius, and Harry's father, so viewers who missed the novel lose the map's whole history. Dana Stevens, reviewing for the New York Times, noted that "a few plot points have been sacrificed" and that Cuarón "does not seem to care much for Quidditch." The book's Quidditch season compresses into one rain-soaked match. And the swap of Dumbledores reads as a loss for anyone attached to the first two films: Gambon is earthier and quicker, with none of Harris's twinkle.

## Prisoner of Azkaban ending explained

Spoiler-free summary first. Harry survives the year with a family he did not have at the start. The mechanics behind that ending are where the film keeps its best plot.

**Spoiler warning. The rest of this section explains the ending in full.**

Peter Pettigrew is the hinge. Scabbers, Ron's missing rat, is Pettigrew in animal form, the friend who betrayed Harry's parents to Voldemort and framed Sirius for the murders. Sirius is innocent, Harry is his godfather, and Lupin is the third of the four friends who made the map. The full moon rises as the group leaves the shack, Lupin transforms into the werewolf his monthly absences hid, and the escape collapses: Pettigrew slips away, and the dementors catch up with Sirius and Harry over the lake.

Hermione's time-turner, a loan from McGonagall for taking too many classes at once, rewinds the pair three hours. From the other side of the afternoon, the pebbles through Hagrid's window are them, the axe falls on a pumpkin, and Buckbeak walks free. The dementor swarm is survived because a Patronus rises across the lake, and Harry waits for his father to send it before working out that his father is dead and casting the stag himself. Sirius escapes on Buckbeak, Lupin resigns by morning, and the Firebolt arrives in the last scene with no sender attached. Harry flies it into the sky, the franchise's lightest final image.
