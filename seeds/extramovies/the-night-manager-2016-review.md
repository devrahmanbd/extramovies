The Night Manager is the rare adaptation that improves on its source by picking one idea and dressing it perfectly: the spy as a beautiful object. Tom Hiddleston plays Jonathan Pine, an ex-soldier running a Cairo hotel who walks into British intelligence and out into the arms trade, and Susanne Bier films the whole thing like a luxury ad with a body count. Rotten Tomatoes has season 1 at 91% with an 8.4/10 average from 67 critics, and the Emmys agreed: the miniseries won two. When the BBC brought it back in January 2026 after a ten-year gap, season 2 landed at 86%.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** A modern spy classic, and the ten-year-old one still holds. Start with the 2016 season, then decide for yourself about the return. Worth watching for Hiddleston and Hugh Laurie alone.

## What The Night Manager is about

John le Carré's 1993 novel gets an update from David Farr, who relocates the arms-trading conspiracy to the present and makes Pine a hotel night manager rather than an employee of the trade. His target is Richard Onslow Roper (Hugh Laurie), a British arms dealer whose charity work launders his inventory, and whose inner circle Pine must join from the inside. Angela Burr (Olivia Colman) runs the operation from London, and the season spends six episodes building the seduction of a man who sells weapons to both sides.

Season 2, written by Farr and directed by Georgi Banks-Davies, picks Pine up in 2026 living under an alias at a low-key MI6 surveillance post in London, with Diego Calva, Camila Morrone, Indira Varma, Paul Chahidi and Hayley Squires joining the cast. Hugh Laurie stays on as an executive producer rather than in front of the camera.

## Cast and performances

Hiddleston is the most charming, intelligent, seductive spy on television, as the Sydney Morning Herald's Brad Newsome put it, and the show is built to prove the point: Pine loses the one fistfight he gets into and wins with information and posture. Laurie answers him as Roper, genial and menacing in the same register, and Colman's Burr gives the tradecraft its humor. The supporting bench, from Alistair Petrie's Sandy Langbourne to Elizabeth Debicki in season 1, is cast like an ensemble drama rather than an action show.

## What works

The texture. Bier shoots Cairo, London, and the Mediterranean like locations in a travel documentary that happen to host a conspiracy, and Victor Reyes' score stays out of the way. The le Carré bones give every scene a second reading: the charity gala is a product showcase, the hotel is a staging ground, and the romance carries operational weight. Rotten Tomatoes' critics consensus for season 1 gets it right: "The Night Manager's smart writing and riveting story are elevated all the more by Hugh Laurie and Tom Hiddleston's captivating performances." Metacritic scored it 82 out of 100, universal acclaim.

## What doesn't

The pace is deliberate to a fault in the back half of season 1, and the gunplay is staged without conviction, deliberately or not. Season 2 carries a different problem: a ten-year gap means the machinery recalibrates before it re-engages, and the RT audience reviews run mixed on the new supporting roster. The consensus for season 2 defends it well enough: "a glamorous and haunted continuation that only gets better as it goes," at 86% with a 7.9/10 average from 50 critics and a 75 on Metacritic.

## What audiences say

Viewer memory treats season 1 as an event: RT audience reviews from repeat watchers still call it one of the best spy thrillers they have seen, and the show's rewatch culture keeps it in le Carré conversations a decade on. Season 2 arrived more guarded, with audience reviews split between those carried by Hiddleston's performance and those who found the new plot thread inert. That is the honest shape of the return: critics 86%, audiences arguing.

## Verdict

Is The Night Manager worth watching? Both seasons, yes, in that order. Season 1 remains one of the great miniseries of the 2010s, and its two Emmy wins and 91% critics score were not nostalgia even then. Season 2 is the quieter achievement, less a continuation than a second look at the same man older. For a genre that usually trades in noise, this is espionage as fine tailoring.
