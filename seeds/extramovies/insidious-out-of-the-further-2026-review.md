Insidious: Out of the Further is worth watching for its two best set pieces and its lead, and for very little else. Jacob Chase's sixth Insidious arrives in US cinemas on August 21, 2026 and keeps the machinery turning. Amelia Eve plays a dental hygienist who walks into The Further in her sleep and can drag spirits back with her. The pillow-fort sequence in the second act is genuinely unnerving. The characters never get there, and by the last act the mythology has been replaced by set dressing.

The money tells a different story. The Numbers has the film at $66,092,129 domestic and $170,638,859 worldwide against an $18 million production budget, with $25,106,020 of the domestic total arriving in the opening weekend. People paid to be scared. Critics did not agree they were. Rotten Tomatoes has the Tomatometer at 57 percent from 136 reviews, and Metacritic sits at 47 from 26 critic reviews.

## What Insidious: Out of the Further is about

Gemma Hall (Amelia Eve) is a dental hygienist who raises her daughter Maya (Island Austin) alone in the house where she grew up. The film opens on a flashback to 1999, where Gemma watches her mother Ingrid (Laura Gordon) lose her mind and kill herself in the bathroom, and where a door closes on its own. Twenty years on, Gemma is having the same kind of dreams, and three elderly neighbours have started appearing on her street.

The dreams are trips into the Further, the purgatory where the franchise keeps its demons and its lost souls. Gemma can do something nobody else in these films can do. She can walk in and bring things out. A cult leader called Cyrus (Sam Spruell) wants that gift, because it means a door between the Further and the living world. He has three acolytes on her street, and their job is to wear Gemma down until she is angry enough to be used. Nick (Brandon Perea) is the cop boyfriend, and Clare (Maisie Richardson-Sellers), who runs a tattoo parlour and can astral project, is the ally who knows what is happening. Elise Rainier (Lin Shaye) turns up with her lantern to explain the rules, and she brings news of the Lamberts. Closing their red door released something, and that something is Cyrus.

## Cast and performances

Eve carries the film, and the script gives her almost nothing to carry it with. She plays a woman who is frightened in her sleep, and she does it with her shoulders. The New York Times called it the sixth installment of a franchise "that has long been losing creative steam", and said the series' reputation "has declined with each new attempt at jump scares". Empire's Becky Darke noticed that the scares around her "rival the iconic shock of the Lipstick-face Demon from the first film", and she singled out the one sequence that works better than anything else in the picture: "A pillow-fort sequence is a standout", all panicked claustrophobia and spooky peeking faces. The Austin Chronicle's Richard Whittaker agreed, calling it the one memorable set piece in a film "overwhelmed by a combination of overly-familiar shocks and a huge amount of gross-out mouth trauma."

Shaye is the other reason to stay. Scott Tobias in The Guardian wrote that "whatever sliver of infectious silliness still remains in this franchise is owed entirely to her", and Empire's Darke called it "always a pleasure to have Lin Shaye back as Elise", the franchise's stalwart paranormal investigator now offering guidance from the other side. Darke also named the problem with the rest of the cast: "Maisie Richardson-Sellers' Clare is saddled with the lazy 'magical negro' archetype", and Clare gets short shrift, including a baffling heist-planning scene involving a punnet of tattooed grapefruits.

Spruell gives Cyrus menace, but the character is a costume more than a mind. RogerEbert.com's Simon Abrams described him as "a tall, gaunt death cult leader who resembles a cross between Marilyn Manson and Todd Rundgren", and Frank Scheck in The Hollywood Reporter took a harder line, saying that after a while the film's sustained creepiness "gives way to self-parody, with all the grotesquely made-up performers portraying the demonic figures starting to look like exhausted Comic-Con attendees after a very long weekend."

## What works

The pillow fort. It is the best thing in the film and it is what makes the rest of the runtime bearable. The whole sequence happens inside a living-room blanket construction, in tight quarters, with something peering in through the gaps.

The sound. The film's loudest asset. There is a dentist's chair scare that uses the room's own acoustics, and the extended sequences in the Further are built on close-mic'd breathing and movement rather than on music.

Shaye. She is eighty-plus and still the most watchable person on screen, and Tobias is right that the franchise owes her the last of its charm.

Eve and Austin. Maya is a child who watches her mother disappear into sleep and come back wrong, and Austin plays that without a line of exposition.

## What doesn't

The mythology. Tobias wrote that the film "mostly goes through the motions" and "arrives as the sixth entry in the series, and the fun has almost completely evaporated, replaced by a dull mythology that would be hard to piece together even if the sequels hadn't come out in scrambled chronology." Abrams made the same point more bluntly: the film is "stretched well past its high concept's breaking point", and the new story follows "a haunted dental hygienist and her worried daughter, albeit without much grounding interest in their characters' or the plot's development."

The characters. Nobody in the film has an interior life that survives a close-up. Gemma is defined by her gift, Maya by her fear, and Cyrus by the script's need for a monster. The acolytes exist only to be irritating.

The back half. Once the plot turns into a chase between realms, the film trades dread for noise. Ryan Lattanzio in IndieWire summed it up: "Direly, dreadfully boring", a movie that "has money on the brain".

The formula. The parent's trauma and the child at risk, the house, the doorway, the ritual. That is a template, and the film never puts anything new inside it.

## Verdict

Is Insidious: Out of the Further worth watching? If you want the franchise's scare machinery in one place and you can sit through a plot that treats you like a beginner, yes. If you want characters who act like people or a mythology you can follow, no. Rotten Tomatoes' consensus says that the film's "modest efforts to put a fresh spin on Insidious only serve to underline how formulaic this franchise has become, though some chilling set pieces distinguish it as one of the most effective entries yet". The 2.71 on Letterboxd says the people who logged the film mostly came away unimpressed. Chase made a competent horror picture with one great scene in it. A $170 million worldwide gross says a lot of people bought a ticket first.

## Spoiler warning: the ending of Insidious: Out of the Further

Cyrus's method is now clear. The three people on Gemma's street are projectors, and their job is to keep Gemma angry and exhausted until she cannot think. Elise, speaking through Clare, lays out the plan instead. Get Cyrus back behind the red door. The red door is not far away. It is behind the closet in the house Gemma grew up in, and it opens onto the bathroom where Ingrid died in 1999.

That is where the film turns Gemma's family history into the plot. Her mother could astral project too. She was a courier like her daughter, and Cyrus hunted her for years, and she killed herself in 1999 rather than let him use Gemma. Gemma finds Ingrid's spirit in the Further and apologises for hating her as a child. Elise also tells her that the gift skips a generation and lands in Maya, who is starting to astral project on her own.

Maya is the one Cyrus gets to. He reaches the living world through the child and starts using her to pull the dead across, and she weakens with every transfer. Nick has to wake the acolytes so that the projectors stop, Elise takes over bodies as needed, and Gemma has to get back to her own body to reach Maya. When the two women collide, Cyrus gets the better of the exchange for a while.

Gemma's answer is the simplest one in the film. She drags her house into the Further with Cyrus and his crowd inside it, and the spirits he promised to raise turn on him. Elise and Ingrid wake Lester's body. Gemma comes back to her own body and to Maya, and the house comes back with her, which means the red door has gone with it.

The film's last image is Cyrus, strapped to a dental chair in the Further with a dentist working on his teeth. He killed himself once to prove he could return from the dead. He has been left in the one place he cannot leave.
