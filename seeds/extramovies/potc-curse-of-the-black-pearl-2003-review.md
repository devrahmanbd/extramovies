The Curse of the Black Pearl is the movie that bet a major studio's fortune on a genre Hollywood had stopped making, and Johnny Depp is the reason the bet paid. Gore Verbinski's 2003 opener runs 143 minutes of rope and cannon smoke around a performance nobody has matched since. Rotten Tomatoes has it at 79% from 212 critics with an 86% audience score, Metacritic reads 63 from 40 critics, and IMDb users give it 8.1/10 from 1.3 million votes, all checked November 10, 2026. Twenty-three years later, it is still the pirate film studios learned from.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** A blockbuster with one genuinely great performance. Verbinski stages every chase and sword fight so the geography stays readable, Geoffrey Rush is a properly nasty mutineer captain, and Depp's Jack Sparrow, the performance behind his 2004 Oscar nomination, still carries the picture. Worth the rewatch.

## What Curse of the Black Pearl is about

Jack Sparrow (Johnny Depp) lost his ship, the Black Pearl, to a mutiny years before the film opens, and he pulls into Port Royal on a boat that sinks beneath him at the dock. The Pearl's new captain, Barbossa (Geoffrey Rush), sails in an hour later to raid the town and takes the governor's daughter, Elizabeth Swann (Keira Knightley), because she carries a coin from a cursed Aztec hoard. Blacksmith Will Turner (Orlando Bloom), who wants her back, breaks a pirate out of jail to give himself a boat that can keep up. The pirate he frees is Jack, and the alliance works only as long as both men need it to.

The curse is the picture's engine. The mutineers spent the treasure, and under moonlight every one of them turns to bone. The antagonist fleet is a costume drama and a monster movie at once, and the repayment terms, coins back in the chest and a blood debt settled, are what the finale is built around.

Per TMDB, the film runs 143 minutes, opened on July 9, 2003, was produced for $140 million, and grossed $655,011,224 worldwide.

The genre history gives the bet its size. In 1995, Cutthroat Island went out with a $115 million budget and came home with almost none of it, per Den of Geek's accounting of the failure, and Hollywood stopped financing pirate pictures. Eight years is how long the genre stayed dark, and Disney reopened the account with Verbinski's film. The Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences then gave it nominations for Makeup, Sound Editing, and Sound Mixing at the 2004 ceremony, and put Depp into the Actor in a Leading Role field.

## Cast and performances

Jack Sparrow's entrance sets the terms. The man arrives standing on the mast of a boat that sinks under him, and he steps off dry onto the dock as it goes down. Depp plays the rest of the movie in that register: a showman who treats every setback as material. The performance is built out of mannerisms that should not work on their own, and the Oscar nomination is the receipt that the combination did.

Rush gives the film its villain, and he plays wicked as fun. Barbossa enjoys his own speeches, and the moonlight scene that first shows his crew as skeletons is the villain's reveal, played by Rush as a man showing off. Bloom and Knightley carry the earnest line of the story. Jack Davenport gives the Royal Navy's Commodore a formal stiffness the harbor plot can use, and Jonathan Pryce's governor is exactly the kind of background official the raid needs.

## What works

Verbinski stages action for geography. Every set piece has an address you can hold in your head: the Port Royal raid moves from street to fort in one legible ascent, and the harbor chase that follows plays out between two ships whose positions you always know. The film spends its $140 million on ships and rigs, and the money shows up on screen.

The curse converts to imagery whenever moonlight cuts into a scene, and the moment the mutineers turn to bone mid-fight is still the image the film is remembered by. Verbinski's staging keeps the conversion readable, which is the technical achievement underneath the franchise's success. The quieter stretches hold their own too: the film gives Depp and Rush scenes together that run on performance rather than spectacle, and both land.

The economics completed the argument. A $140 million production grossing $655,011,224 worldwide, per TMDB, turned a genre Hollywood had abandoned into an account worth reopening, and the studio acted on it within three years.

## What doesn't

The middle loops. After the harbor escape, the plot cycles through capture and pursuit before it deposits everyone at the finale's cave, and at 143 minutes the repetition shows. Will and Elizabeth's thread also gets a handful of beats, and the closing scene presents the two as a couple on the strength of very little development.

The comedy repeats a few of Sparrow's physical gags, and the connective tissue between set pieces runs long. The critics' numbers show where the film divided them: the Tomatometer held at 79% while Metacritic settled at 63 from 40 reviews. The criticism lands on real things.

## What audiences say

The audience verdict has been the steadier half. Rotten Tomatoes' Popcornmeter runs 86%, IMDb holds 8.1/10 from 1.3 million votes, and TMDB carries 7.8 from 23,060 votes, all checked November 10, 2026. That is a live audience score for a film two decades past its theatrical run.

## Verdict

Is Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl worth watching? Yes. Verbinski put $140 million on screen in readable form, Rush and Depp run the film as a double act between a villain who loves his job and a hero who refuses to behave, and the ending pays off the curse. The deductions are real: the middle loops, the romance is thin, and some of the noise is padding. The film still makes its genre case in every set piece, and 1.3 million IMDb users have kept agreeing with it for twenty-three years.
