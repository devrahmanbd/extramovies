Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows: Part 2 earns its ending. David Yates directs the saga's shortest film at its highest pitch: a 130-minute sprint from a seaside grave to the ruins of a burning school, staged in corridors and collapsing staircases, scored by Alexandre Desplat at his most mournful. Rotten Tomatoes' ranking of the wizarding-world franchise puts this finale first, at 96 percent, and Metacritic's 85 from 41 critics is the room's verdict too, both checked November 10, 2026. The film's flaws are real and located: a post-converted 3D job that dims the picture, and an epilogue played at half the film's intelligence. Everything else is payoff, and the payoff is why this remains the highest-demand single Harry Potter review on search. It opened wide in the US on July 15, 2011, and its $1.34 billion worldwide per TMDB made it the saga's biggest earner.

## What Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows: Part 2 is about

Voldemort holds the Elder Wand, taken from Dumbledore's tomb, and the film opens where Part 1 closed: Harry stands at the grave that reads "A Free Elf", the trio bargains with the goblin Griphook, and the plan is one last heist. Bellatrix's vault inside Gringotts hides a Horcrux, the escape runs out of the bank on the back of a half-ton dragon, and from there the story turns toward Hogwarts, where the remaining Horcruxes wait behind its walls. Voldemort's army arrives at the school's borders before dawn. The battle takes the rest of the picture.

Steve Kloves wrote the screenplay, his seventh adaptation of Rowling's eight novels, and David Yates directed his fourth and last, all credits verified on TMDB and Wikipedia's film page. Eduardo Serra shot the film in the same ash-grey palette he gave Part 1, and Mark Day cut it to the series' leanest runtime.

## The Battle of Hogwarts, staged

Yates shoots the battle as a chain of close decisions rather than one wide-screen melee. The Great Hall becomes a field hospital with the wounded laid out on benches. Maggie Smith's McGonagall animates the stone statues to hold the courtyard and gets the film's one broad laugh out of the order: "I've always wanted to use that spell." Matthew Lewis' Neville, promoted across eight films from running joke to the student who leads the defense, lures Death Eaters onto the wooden bridge and brings it down on them. The camera keeps returning to faces: two boys at a window, a death that happens in silence at the edge of a frame, fire crossing a courtyard overhead. The spectacle is enormous and the staging stays legible, which is the trick most blockbusters of the decade fumbled.

## The Snape scene and Desplat's score

The film's center is a death. In the boathouse, Nagini strikes Snape down, and he gives Harry his memories with one request: "Look at me." The Pensieve scene that follows resolves a decade of menace into grief, and the doe Patronus that carries his private history away with it is the series' single best sound cue. Alan Rickman died in 2016, and this scene is the reason his Snape ranks among the great screen antagonists: eight films of withholding, paid off in five minutes. Ralph Fiennes gets his best Voldemort work here too, quieter and stranger in the King's Cross limbo scene opposite Michael Gambon's Dumbledore.

Desplat scored both halves of Deathly Hallows after John Williams, composer of the first three films' scores, was unavailable for the finale, per Wikipedia's production history. His writing is choral and cold where the early films' themes were playful, and he lets the series' shared language surface only where the story can afford a memory of warmth.

## What doesn't

The 3D. This was the series' first 3D release, converted in post-production, and the conversion costs the picture real contrast. Paste's Jonathan Hickman wrote at the time that so much of the film looks out of focus and the technology is "not worth paying for." SBS's review disagreed and praised the depth, so critics split; the honest advice is to find a 2D screening where the ash-grey palette reads as intended.

The epilogue. The "nineteen years later" coda at King's Cross sends the grown trio's children to Platform 9 3/4 through makeup and soft light, and Den of Geek's contemporaneous review caught the problem exactly: closing moments that "reach for poignancy, but achieve an odd combination of feeling both interminable and strangely rushed." The film knows how to end. It appends a farewell card anyway.

The book assumption. Kloves' screenplay moves like a checklist of the novel's remaining business, and viewers who skipped Part 1 or the book will meet the Hallows lore at full speed. The film trusts its audience to have done the reading. It is the rare finale where that trust feels like a feature, but the barrier is real on a first watch.

## What audiences say

The aggregate picture, checked November 10, 2026: Rotten Tomatoes' Tomatometer stands at 96 percent from 333 critics with an 8.3 average, first among the wizarding-world films on the site's own ranking. Metacritic carries a Metascore of 85 from 41 critics and a user score of 8.2 from about 1,800 ratings. IMDb shows 8.1 from more than a million users. TMDB has 8.1 from 22,721 votes. CinemaScore audiences graded the film an A at release, and the Broadcast Film Critics Association's 93 made it that group's highest-rated Harry Potter film, per Wikipedia's tallies.

Read the numbers correctly and they agree with each other: this is the saga's consensus peak, rare for a finale, and the praise clusters on the same things a rewatch rewards, the battle's staging and Rickman's scene.

## Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows: Part 2's ending, explained

Everything above is safe to read before watching. What follows gives away the ending in full.

The memories Snape gives Harry reveal the last secret: Voldemort's killing curse on baby Harry left a fragment of the Dark Lord's soul inside the boy, so Harry must die before Voldemort can be killed. He walks into the forest alone, opens the snitch's third turn to the Resurrection Stone, and meets his parents' ghosts for the walk. The curse kills the fragment, and the film's strangest, best scene follows in a white-limbo King's Cross, where Dumbledore explains the choice Harry now has to go back. He takes it.

The battle's final turn belongs to Neville: Voldemort forces the flaming Sorting Hat onto his head, and Neville pulls the Sword of Gryffindor out of it to behead Nagini, the last Horcrux, with Voldemort watching. That leaves the Elder Wand, which Voldemort believes is his. Harry explains what the film has seeded twice: the wand's allegiance moved from Dumbledore to Draco Malfoy to Harry, so it will not strike its true master. Harry breaks it in two and throws the pieces off the bridge, a change from the novel, where Harry keeps the wand, and the film's version is the cleaner moral close.

The coda names the cost and the grace: Albus Severus Potter, named for two headmasters, one of them Snape, whose loyalty the whole series has just reread.

## Verdict

Is Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows: Part 2 worth watching? Yes. It closes a ten-film saga on its best-reviewed entry, with a battle staged in fire and stone, a boathouse scene that turns a decade of menace into grief, and a score that holds both. The 3D conversion is skippable and the epilogue is the film's one sentimental misstep. Metacritic's 85 and the 96 percent on the Tomatometer capture the consensus. The 9 here captures the bridge Neville drops and the word "Always."
