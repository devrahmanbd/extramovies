## What Tumbbad is about

Tumbbad opens with a warning delivered over a village drowning in monsoon rain, then follows Vinayak Rao, the son of a servant household cast out of a wealthy estate. Years later he comes back to the village of Tumbbad in Maharashtra, drawn by a rumour his family never let go of: gold is sealed in the courtyard of an old goddess statue, and something else guards it.

The something else is Hastar, a god nobody prays to. The film's opening narration lays out his origin: the firstborn child of the creator, handed dominion over gold and grain, then cursed by his own siblings and driven out of heaven. He lives in the statue's womb. He offers gold to anyone willing to descend, and he attacks anyone who takes more than they should.

Vinayak descends. He learns the rules, builds his own method for getting out alive, and starts shipping gold out of Tumbbad in bulk. The story runs in chapters, ending in 1947, and each chapter tightens the same idea: the gold is never free, the debt is always paid in blood, and the person paying it changes.

## Is Tumbbad based on a true story

The village is real. Tumbbad is a settlement in Maharashtra, and the film was shot in and around it, which is why the mud, the courtyard and the temple steps feel documented rather than built.

Hastar is not real. The god, the curse and the rules of the courtyard were written for the film by Rahi Anil Barve with writers Adesh Prasad and Ani Gautam, working from a story Barve developed with Pushkar and Gayatri. There is no folk religion behind him, which is exactly what makes the movie unsettling: it invents a deity with the confidence of an inherited one.

The invention also solves a practical problem. Folklore horror usually inherits its rules, so an audience arrives already knowing how the creature behaves, and Tumbbad cannot rely on any of that. It has to teach you its own rules in real time, and every lesson costs Vinayak something, which keeps the exposition inside the danger instead of outside it.

## Why the Tumbbad movie looks different from other Indian horror

Three craft choices carry the film.

First, the rain. Tumbbad is wet almost the entire way through, and the monsoon does the work a jump scare usually does. Sound arrives before the threat, the courtyard floods, and the gold sits in water the whole time, which turns every descent into a race against weather as much as against Hastar.

Second, the colour. The palette runs from mud brown to furnace orange, and the only thing that ever glows is the gold itself. When the film wants you to feel temptation, it does not need dialogue, it just lets the metal light up a face.

Third, the performance. Sohum Shah produced the film over roughly a decade and plays Vinayak from an eager young man to a hoarse, limping addict. Mohammad Samad, who plays the young Pandurang, gets the film's moral weight, and the scenes between father and son in the final chapter work because the boy has been watching the whole time.

Fourth, the repetition of the route itself. Each trip down follows the same beats: the rope, the bucket, the same drop taken over and over until the path reads as ritual rather than exploration. By the final chapter you have watched Vinayak perform the descent enough times that the camera no longer needs to explain the space, and the horror arrives on a familiar walk instead of an unfamiliar one. That economy is why the climax can hold two generations in one courtyard without cutting away to anything else.

It is worth saying what Tumbbad does not have: a single sustained antagonist of the slasher kind. The horror comes from appetite. ## Tumbbad ending explained

![Tumbbad ending explained: Pandurang walks away from the cursed village in the Tumbbad poster](https://image.tmdb.org/t/p/w500/z1xOCxw780WFJC5uCTMfCkQ4Agi.jpg)

The final chapter is set in 1947. Vinayak, old now and still hungry, brings his son Pandurang to Tumbbad to secure the gold for the next generation. He has done this before, with rules and rope and the dough doll he has used since boyhood as a decoy to keep Hastar busy while he fills his pockets.

This time the courtyard has changed. Hastar has multiplied: the god is now a swarm of copies, and the chamber crawls with them. When the swarm comes for both of them, Vinayak does the only thing left to do. He ties the dough dolls to his own body and lets every clone tear into him so that Pandurang can climb out.

Vinayak follows, burnt, half blind and cursed, exactly like the scarred old woman who once guarded the courtyard and warned him what it would cost. In the film's last beat he holds out the loincloth of gold to his son, repeating the offer his own father made. Pandurang refuses it. He tells his father to sleep, invokes Hastar's name to put him under, and then burns him where he lies. The boy walks out of Tumbbad and does not look back.

That refusal is the whole film. The curse is not broken by a spell or a temple ritual. It ends because one person in the line declines the gold, and Tumbbad's horror is that it took greed, exhaustion and a dead father to get him to that decision. The sequel in development picks up with Pandurang as the adult heir, which is the story the ending sets in motion.

## The verdict

Tumbbad is a slow, wet, beautifully ugly fable that treats greed as the actual monster, and it lands. The TMDB rating of 7.6 from 373 votes matches the consensus around it, and the 86% Tomatometer from 28 reviews confirms critics saw the same film.
