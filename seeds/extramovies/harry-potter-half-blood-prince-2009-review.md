Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince is the saga's most beautiful film and its most divided attention. David Yates directs the sixth chapter as a dusk-soaked school romance running against the darkest business the story has yet conducted, and the halves never agree on which film they are in. The critics' room forgave the split: Rotten Tomatoes' consensus calls it "dark, thrilling, and occasionally quite funny" and "visually stunning and emotionally satisfying," at 83 percent from 279 reviews, and Metacritic carries a 78 from 36 critics, both checked November 12, 2026. The accounting is honest: Bruno Delbonnel's photography earned the series' only Academy Award nomination for Best Cinematography, Jim Broadbent's Slughorn is the best new character performance six films in, and the romance subplot eats the middle of the picture.

## What Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince is about

Voldemort's war has reached past the school gates. The film opens with the Millennium Bridge folding under a Death Eater attack, and by term's start the official reassurances have collapsed into open fear. Dumbledore collects Horace Slughorn, a retired potions master with a selective memory, because Slughorn once taught a gifted student named Tom Riddle and carries the memory explaining how the boy cheated death. Harry, armed with an annotated textbook signed "the Half-Blood Prince", works that memory out of him while the war closes on the castle.

The second thread is the one the marketing ran on. The trio is sixteen, and the screenplay spends a large share of its runtime on crushes and love potions: Harry orbiting Ginny Weasley, Ron stuck to Lavender Brown by chocolates meant for someone else. Tom Felton's Draco runs the dark half from the margins, repairing a vanishing cabinet while the school worries about Quidditch trials.

## The craft: dusk with a purpose

Delbonnel treats the whole picture as an experiment in failing light. His stated method was to float the light so it sometimes reached faces and sometimes hid them, and the result shows in sequences any viewer can name: the orphanage where Tom Riddle spent his childhood, Slughorn's classroom steaming in copper light, the cave where Dumbledore's face keeps disappearing into the dark. The film remains the saga's only Best Cinematography nominee, per the Academy's own ceremony record. Nicholas Hooper scores the comedy and the dread with the same muted instruments.

## What works

Broadbent's Slughorn is the film's best performance and the best new character the franchise had introduced in six films. He is vain and cowardly where it counts, genuinely fond of his favorites, and the film's whole strategy is a dinner party: Dumbledore and Harry court him over a feast and wait for the memory he is ashamed to give up.

The cave sequence is the scariest stretch the saga had staged: a basin of potion that can only be emptied by drinking it, Dumbledore's instruction that Harry must force him to drink no matter what he begs, and the Inferi rising out of the black water, staged almost entirely in faces. It plays like a horror film because it is one. Variety's Todd McCarthy called the film "dazzlingly well made" and noted it plays "in a mode closer to palpable life-or-death drama" than its predecessors, per Metacritic's archive. Tom Felton's Draco gets the year's best actor's business: the bathroom duel is the series' first on-screen blood, and he plays the assignment as a boy dissolving under a debt his family cannot pay.

## What doesn't

The see-saw is the film's structural bill. The Horcrux hunt advances through exactly two collected memories on screen, the orphanage and Slughorn's stolen one, while the novel's Gaunt chapters, the family tree that explains Voldemort completely, are gone entirely. A book reader feels the subtraction; a first-time viewer mostly feels the plot idling while the cast flirts. The film runs 153 minutes and the war's business occupies less of them than a Quidditch subplot.

The title mystery is demoted to a homework tool, parked until the last chase, and it gets less screen time than a love-potion farce. The ending is rushed too: the film answers the novel's funeral with one silent wand salute in the Great Hall, a beautiful image of grief that lasts about a minute. The patience the death deserved went to the Burrow attack, a Christmas sequence invented for the film that burns the Weasley house down and is never mentioned again.

## Half-Blood Prince ending explained

Spoiler-free summary first. Harry survives the year, the school does not end it intact, and the film closes on a choice that sends Harry away from Hogwarts for good. The mechanics sit behind the marker.

**Spoiler warning. The rest of this section explains the ending in full.**

Harry and Dumbledore return from the cave with the headmaster poisoned by the potion he drank to reach the locket, and the Dark Mark hangs over the castle. They fly to the Astronomy Tower, where Dumbledore, wandless and weak, freezes Harry under his own cloak before the visitor arrives. It is Draco Malfoy, sent to finish what his father could not, and the scene's terror is watching a sixth-year stall with a killing assignment while Death Eaters climb through the windows behind him. Draco disarms Dumbledore and hesitates. Snape does not. The killing curse sends Dumbledore over the tower's edge.

The scene plants the finale's logic while it kills him. Draco's Expelliarmus strips the Elder Wand from Dumbledore, and wand allegiance is the machinery Deathly Hallows builds its ending on. The locket Dumbledore died reaching for is fake: a note inside signs off R.A.B., and the film banks the initials for Part 2. The chase after gives the title its answer: Harry pursues Snape across the grounds, fires his annotated spells, and Snape deflects them with the line that closes the mystery. He invented the spells in the book. He is the Half-Blood Prince.

The last scene is the salute. The four houses raise their wands, the lights dissolve into the sky, and Harry tells Ron and Hermione he is not coming back to school. ## The verdict

Is Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince worth watching? Yes, with the balance problem priced in. The case for it is Delbonnel's photography and Broadbent's Slughorn, plus a cave sequence the saga never bettered for dread. The case against it is on the screen too: the romance subplot takes the engine out of the Horcrux hunt, the title mystery idles until the last five minutes, and the film's grief for Dumbledore runs about a minute against a love-potion farce that runs all year. Metacritic's 78 and the 83 percent on the Tomatometer say the critics' room forgave the see-saw. The 7.0 here counts what the see-saw cost.
