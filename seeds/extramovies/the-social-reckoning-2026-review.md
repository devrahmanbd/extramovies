Yes, The Social Reckoning is worth watching, and mostly for the two people in it. Aaron Sorkin wrote, produced and directed, and the result is a 113-minute whistleblower procedural that argues a case the audience finished years ago. Mikey Madison and Jeremy Allen White carry it, Jeff Cronenweth photographs it, and Alexandre Desplat scores it, but the film is loud where it needed to be quiet. Rotten Tomatoes has it at 54 percent from 250 critic reviews and Metacritic at 51 from 58, both checked October 21, 2026.

## What The Social Reckoning is about

Frances Haugen (Mikey Madison) is a product manager on Facebook's short-lived civic integrity team in 2019. The film opens on Kayleigh (Portia Doubleday), a motorist whose confrontation with two police officers on the side of a road is recorded on her phone and posted under the header "Voice of Reason," a white-supremacist group on Facebook. Haugen recognises Kayleigh as a woman she knew back when she was a mellow progressive, and her research has already produced the number that drives the film: 64 percent of Facebook users who joined extremist groups were sent there by the platform's own recommendation tool.

She needs a journalist. Jeff Horwitz (Jeremy Allen White) has just moved from the Washington politics beat to the Wall Street Journal's San Francisco technology beat, where his first problem is a car GPS he cannot operate. He writes letters to almost every Facebook employee. Haugen answers one. They meet in a park, she is anxious about the NDA she has signed, and what starts there plays as a stealth romantic comedy, with Haugen in striking black cocktail dresses and an explanation of the outfit that makes anyone who sees her in a bar assume she is on a date. Matt Zoller Seitz in RogerEbert.com noticed that pattern, and the film's "stealth rom-com" structure, before most of the press did.

The story runs from 2019 to 2021, and the timeline the film puts along the bottom of the screen reaches back to 2018, so the viewer always knows where they are. A glossary of internal acronyms carries the corporate machinery: MSI for Meaningful Social Interactions, CIT for the Civic Integrity Team, SATF for the Soft Action Task Force. Zuckerberg (Jeremy Strong) will not approve a safeguard that costs one percent of engagement. His line about the platform is "We're a corkboard. We don't control what people pin on it." On January 6, 2021, his staff wait for hours before he will order the insurrection videos taken down, and he calls the deletion a last-resort hard action. The investigation produces a run of Wall Street Journal exposés called the Facebook Files.

## Cast and performances

Madison plays Haugen, and the film is better for it. David Rooney in The Hollywood Reporter called it "a smart choice for her biggest role since bagging an Oscar for Anora" and put the film's heart in one line: "the heart of the movie is White and especially Madison."

White's Horwitz is a man whose decency is the engine. His first mistake at the Journal is an inability to work the GPS in his own car, and his first big story comes from a source he has to earn. Strong plays Mark Zuckerberg, and it is the most divisive performance in the film. Matt Zoller Seitz in RogerEbert.com found a "rotting Shakespearean prince whose one-track brain roams all over the spectrum, treating humor as an offensive weapon not be used except in combat." Other critics read the same coldness as a dead end, and the film is better when Strong is funny than when he is not.

Betty Gilpin plays Christina, the bureau chief who does not want a politics reporter covering technology. Wunmi Mosaku plays Raya, the crisis manager whose job is to make Zuckerberg likable, and Bill Burr plays Charlie Garrigan, a former Facebook employee turned hostile consultant who has published an op-ed condemning the company's safeguards. Gbenga Akinnagbe plays Andy, the editor who is easier about all of it. Billy Magnussen plays Teddy Marks, the media relations executive who says the company is "not frightened by Congress" because it is "post-government around here."

## What works

The two leads. RogerEbert.com's Matt Zoller Seitz gave the film three stars and still wrote that "it's an involving drama with lots of humor" and that it "could turn out to be a surprise success with audiences." The pairing works because both actors play restraint against Sorkin's noise.

The craft. Jeff Cronenweth shot The Social Network and shot this one, and the camera gives the procedural the clean joints of a network news package. The timeline along the bottom of the screen and the acronyms in the dialogue are the same device in two forms, and the film needs both to keep a story this dense legible.

The score. Desplat's music keeps a talky film moving without the trembling strings that would tell you how to feel about it.

## What doesn't

The timing. Guy Lodge in Variety led with the problem in his first line: where the 2010 film about the founding of Facebook "was almost unnervingly timely," this procedural about the 2021 leak "already feels behind the curve."

The directorial style. Lodge found Sorkin at the helm to be "a televisual stylist at heart" with "nothing approaching Fincher's visual ingenuity, his ability to locate tactile, haunting atmosphere in the sterile, textureless lairs of tech bros and newspapermen alike." Nobody in this film has a private life, and Sorkin's direction prizes dialogue and performance over visuals.

The lecturing. The film runs a timeline along the bottom of the screen and a glossary of internal acronyms, and both are small lectures in a movie that already has characters talking for a living. David Rooney in The Hollywood Reporter noted that "Sorkin's tendency for self-righteous indignation is very much in evidence," and then gave the film its due, since the material "provides him with plenty of legitimate reasons to be pissed, and the movie is never at risk of becoming a pedantic lecture."

## Verdict

Is The Social Reckoning worth watching? Yes, for the two performances and for the argument that social media's damage is a policy problem as much as a content problem. It is also a film that knows its own ending and spends 113 minutes getting there.

Sorkin has made a loud, talky, strangely sincere procedural with two actors at the top of their game, and the case he makes about the engagement metric is one worth making. Critics who wanted a thriller got a lecture, and the film's own verdict on that is the one Guy Lodge reached when he called the whole thing a movie that already feels behind the curve. That is what happens when the warning arrives in 2026.

## Spoiler warning: the ending of The Social Reckoning

The film finishes on Haugen's testimony before the Senate Commerce Committee, and it treats the moment as the one place where the truth gets spoken to power. Guy Lodge in Variety called it "a rare, rousing instance of truth being spoken to power" and noted that the story ties itself off there.

Afterwards, Sorkin mourns what the company has become. Facebook renamed itself Meta, and the film's last word is that nothing changed. No fact-checking arrived, the platform still counts north of three billion monthly active users, and 43 percent of American adults still get their news from it. The name change is the only crumb of hope the film offers, and it is a small one.

The film does not put a victory speech in Haugen's mouth. It puts a hearing on screen and a 43 percent statistic in the last reel.
