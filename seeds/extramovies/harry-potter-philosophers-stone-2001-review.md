Yes, Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone earns a rewatch, and it is the rare franchise opener that knows what a beginning owes the book it adapts. Chris Columbus takes Steve Kloves' faithful screenplay at a patient pace: the cupboard under the stairs, the flood of letters, Hagrid kicking in the door at midnight, the brick wall opening onto Diagon Alley. John Williams' score and Stuart Craig's sets give that patience its spine. Rotten Tomatoes has the film at 80 percent from 196 critics, Metacritic at 65 from 37, TMDB at 7.9 from 30,593 votes, checked November 10, 2026. The final hour strains. Everything before it holds.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** Rewatch it before the HBO series arrives. A warm, faithful, handsomely built first film whose score and design still set the terms every wizarding screen adaptation is judged against. The first hour is close to perfect genre craft. The last thirty minutes fight effects that have aged.

## What Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone is about

Harry Potter has spent eleven years in the cupboard under the stairs of 4 Privet Drive, and the film spends its first act breaking him out of it. Letters arrive in green ink and get burned before he can read them. Hagrid kicks the door in on his birthday and tells him he is a wizard, that his parents did not die in a car crash, and that a place has been waiting for him at Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. Gringotts, Platform 9 3/4 and a wand shop follow, then the Sorting Hat, then a school year that circles one question: which professor serves the villain who killed Harry's parents.

Chris Columbus directs from Steve Kloves' screenplay, shot by cinematographer John Seale and cut by Richard Francis-Bruce, with John Williams scoring the picture: all credits verified on Wikipedia's film page. The film premiered at the Odeon Leicester Square on November 4, 2001, opened wide in the UK and the United States on November 16, and was the highest-grossing release of 2001, with $974 million on first release and past $1 billion after re-releases.

## Cast and performances

Three children carry each other. Daniel Radcliffe plays Harry with a wariness suited to a boy who is unwanted at one address and famous at another; Rupert Grint's Ron supplies the humor; Emma Watson's Hermione is written off at first meeting and turns out to be why the story closes. Richard Harris' Dumbledore is warm the way a headmaster should be. Alan Rickman's Snape gets more menace out of three syllables than most villains get in a monologue. Robbie Coltrane's Hagrid anchors the film emotionally, and USA Today's Claudia Puig singled out his Hagrid for praise alongside the set design in her three-out-of-four-star review at the time. Maggie Smith's McGonagall and Tom Felton's Draco Malfoy set the boarding-school register: adults are institutions, children are rivals waiting to be won over. All ten names sit verified on TMDB's cast list.

## What works

The score. Williams composed it in Los Angeles and Tanglewood and recorded it in London in September 2001, and one of its main themes carried a title of its own: "Hedwig's Theme", which Williams kept in the finished score after "everyone seemed to like it," and which went on to recur across the whole series, per Wikipedia's production history. Twenty-five years on, every rewatch starts with that sound cue.

The design. Stuart Craig built the Hogwarts Great Hall at Leavesden Studios on English cathedral architecture, and when the production offered him an existing old street for Diagon Alley he chose to build his own set from Tudor, Georgian and Queen Anne forms instead, per Wikipedia. Judianna Makovsky re-designed the Quidditch robes, dressing the players in "preppie sweaters, 19th-century fencing breeches and arm guards". Todd McCarthy's Variety review put the craft plainly: "The script is faithful, the actors are just right, the sets, costumes, makeup and effects match and sometimes exceed anything one could imagine." Roger Ebert gave the film four stars, called it "a classic," and singled out the Quidditch effects.

The first hour's patience. The building blocks land one at a time and each gets room to breathe, which is why a movie about a magic school feels built by hand rather than rendered in bulk.

## What doesn't

The length. This is a 152-minute picture and the count shows in the school-term middle, where classroom beats repeat a rhythm the story has already established. Jonathan Foreman of the New York Post called the adaptation "remarkably faithful" and "consistently entertaining if overlong," which remains the fair verdict: the film transcribes the novel's chapter list.

The score, checkered reception. Critics who loved the film pushed back on its volume. Puig wrote that the "overly insistent score lacks subtlety and bludgeons us with crescendos" and worried that "many of the book's readers may wish for a more magical incarnation." The Hollywood Reporter's Kirk Honeycutt called Williams' music "a great clanging, banging music box that simply will not shut up" even as his review praised the sets, design, effects and cast.

The final half hour. The troll fight and the chambers beneath the trapdoor lean on effects that never match the physical sets around them, and critics flagged the stretch at the time. The eight-film saga went on to spend the budget increases that problem needed.

## What audiences say

The aggregate picture, checked November 10, 2026: Rotten Tomatoes' Tomatometer stands at 80 percent from 196 critic reviews and its Popcornmeter at 82 percent from more than 250,000 verified ratings; Metacritic carries a Metascore of 65 from 37 critics, split 68 percent positive and 32 percent mixed, plus a user score of 7.9 from 1,265 ratings; TMDB has 7.9 from 30,593 votes. The film returned to American theaters on February 13, 2025, and Rotten Tomatoes' US gross row reads $317.9 million across runs. The awards record is modest but real: three Oscar nominations for art direction, costume design and Williams' original score, plus seven BAFTA nominations including Best British Film, per Wikipedia's listing.

Read the crowd against the critics correctly. Metacritic's 65 is solid rather than spectacular, and the top score on its critic list belongs to The Guardian's Peter Bradshaw, whose line doubles as the rewatch argument: "It's still a very entertaining and spectacular movie, with a rush of nostalgia to go alongside the exhilaration of fun." The audience side has kept agreeing with him for a quarter century.

## Philosopher's Stone and the eight-film saga

This film opened the run that would grow to eight features across a decade, and the rewatch question has turned practical again: HBO's Harry Potter series is in production at the same Leavesden facility, with Francesca Gardiner developing it, and its premiere window is the one detail the sources do not agree on, so no date promise here. ## Verdict

Is Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone worth watching? Yes, and the case is airtight before the hour mark: a world built by hand where other films render theirs, a score that became the franchise's sound, child leads who grew into the roles. The reservations are real and located: a repetitive middle, a loud mix from the composer its own defenders have to explain, and effects that age across the last thirty minutes. Metacritic's 65 captures that divided room. The 80 percent on the Tomatometer captures the Great Hall's first reveal, and that is the shot that keeps paying out on every rewatch.
