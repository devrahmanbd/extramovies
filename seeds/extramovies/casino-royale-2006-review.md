Casino Royale is the Bond reboot that made the character earn everything on screen. Martin Campbell's 2006 film runs Daniel Craig's 007 from candidate to 00 agent inside one picture and closes with an ending that takes more from him than a mission normally does. Rotten Tomatoes holds it at 94% from 266 critic reviews with a 90% audience score, Metacritic reads 80 from 46 reviews, and IMDb users give it 8.0/10 from around 704,000 votes, all checked November 10, 2026. Twenty years on, it is still the introduction the character needed.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** The Bond film that plays like a thriller. Craig earns the licence to kill in scenes that cost him, the Madagascar chase and the Montenegro card game are as good as the franchise's set pieces get, and the ending pays off the film's tension with interest. Worth all 144 minutes.

## What Casino Royale is about

M sends Bond after Le Chiffre (Mads Mikkelsen), a banker who moves the money of the world's terrorists and has a very large hole in his books. The gap closes only with a big win at a high-stakes poker game in Montenegro, so Bond sits across from him with government money on his stake and Vesper Lynd (Eva Green), the Treasury's representative, keeping score. The film opens in black and white with the two kills that grant Bond his 00 status, and it keeps the whole running time on procedure and consequences.

The reset shows in the film's habits. Bond's mistakes cost him real blood, and the romance is part of the mission structure rather than a breather between set pieces. TMDB lists the James Bond Collection at 27 titles, and the character's modern behavior starts with this entry.

## Cast and performances

Craig plays Bond as a specialist whose reflexes outrun his judgment, and the film marks each point where better judgment would have kept him out of trouble. The performance is physical: the answer to some of the film's questions is a man moving wrong and paying for it. Eva Green gives Vesper the film's interior life. Vesper is the only character allowed to change, and Green makes every scene between her and Bond cost more than the last, which is what the ending needs to land.

Mikkelsen plays Le Chiffre with the script's best idea: a villain who needs the money more than the world needs him stopped. Judi Dench's M runs the film's professional register, Giancarlo Giannini's handler keeps the mission believable, and Jeffrey Wright's Felix Leiter is the one ally whose support Bond can trust without a second reading.

## What works

The Madagascar chase is the set piece the film is known for, and its reputation is earned in the details. Bond chases a bomb-maker through a construction site, the choreography treats every climb as a problem with a solution, and the whole sequence plays with positions you can follow. Most chase scenes cut to reaction shots when they want pace. This one keeps geography and pace in the same frame.

The Montenegro card game is the structural centerpiece, and it earns the runtime the film gives it. The stakes were set earlier in the plot, each big bet lands on someone's finances, and the tension builds from numbers rather than explosions. Vesper's presence makes the table a relationship scene as much as a game, and the film cuts away from it exactly when the plot needs a different kind of danger.

The interrogation stretch is the film's hardest scene and its most honest. Bond sits in a chair with nothing, the villain explains himself without a gadget in sight, and the film asks its audience to sit through what happens to its hero. It is darker than the film's PG-13 certification usually announces, and the craft holds because Campbell shoots the scene without stylistic cues to soften it.

## What doesn't

At 144 minutes the film runs long for a thriller, and the final act loosens the tension the poker night built. The story needs a Venice complication after the table closes, and the scenes that bridge the two halves wander before the plot finds its footing again. Le Chiffre's arc also ends before the film does, which hands the last act to forces the plot introduces late, and the first hour makes a better promise than the third act keeps.

The connective middle runs long on meetings and travel between the two big set pieces, and the villains outside Le Chiffre get less screen time than the mystery around them suggests. Viewers who want the organization explained will leave with questions the film defers to its sequels.

## Verdict

Is Casino Royale worth watching? Yes. It is the Bond film that set the character's modern reflexes, the Madagascar chase and the Montenegro table hold up as set pieces, and the ending still does its damage on a rewatch. The deductions are real: the final act wanders, the villain exits early, and the run time carries a length the first hour never needed. None of the deductions land on Craig, whose work in the part holds the film together from entrance to last scene.
