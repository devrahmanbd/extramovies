Imelda Staunton's Dolores Umbridge is the best villain the Harry Potter films produced, and she rules this movie. Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix trades the tournament spectacle of [Goblet of Fire](/reviews/harry-potter-goblet-of-fire-2005-review) for something the franchise had never attempted: a political thriller set inside a school, where the threat is a ministry takeover carried out with a giggle and a pink cardigan. Rotten Tomatoes carries the film at 78 percent from 255 critics, Metacritic gives it a 71 from 37 critics, and TMDB holds 7.7 from 21,466 votes, all checked November 10, 2026. The compression cuts both ways. What remains is the saga's most adult film and its meanest villain.

> **Spoiler-free verdict:** The political one. Staunton's Umbridge, a secret student army, and a Ministry siege that ends the series' school-era innocence. David Yates compresses the longest book into the shortest film, and the Department of Mysteries delivers the franchise's best battle to that date. Grawp drags, the cramping shows, and the digital grey takes some getting used to.

## What Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix is about

Harry spends the film's opening fighting dementors in a Surrey underpass, then discovers the wizarding world has been told he is a liar. The Ministry of Magic runs a smear campaign against him and Dumbledore, and it installs Umbridge as Defense Against the Dark Arts teacher with a brief to keep the children calm. She teaches theory while Voldemort regroups. Her rule hardens through a stack of Educational Decrees nailed to the wall, a spy network of students, and detentions where the black quill makes Harry carve "I must not tell lies" into his own hand.

The resistance answers with a secret: Dumbledore's Army, a student defense group that meets in the Room of Requirement and learns real spellwork from Harry. The plot threads pull toward the Ministry itself, toward a prophecy locked in the Department of Mysteries that Voldemort wants and Harry needs, and toward the visions that connect the two. Sirius Black, Sirius's money, Snape's Occlumency lessons, and Luna Lovegood's thestrals all feed the same funnel. It is the most plot the series has carried, in the least running time.

## Staunton and the politics

Yates, a television director making his feature debut for the franchise, shoots the Ministry as bureaucracy rather than fantasy. Time Out's review caught the production design's range, from the "Georgian mustiness" of the Order's safehouse to the Ministry's polished tiles, and the hearing where Harry is tried stacks jurors like a court of doges. Slant's Nick Schager described the film's register as dread and powerlessness crushing the series' old buoyancy, and that is the correct reading: this is a film about institutions failing on purpose.

Staunton plays Umbridge from the inside. The flounce, the giggled threats, the kitten plates on the office wall: each decorative choice is a mask over cruelty, and the performance makes the film's point that evil here is administrative. Variety's Todd McCarthy called the film "considerably grimmer and grittier than the previous pictures," and the grimmest instrument in it is a woman with a tea set. Michael Goldenberg's screenplay gives her the line readings; Staunton gives the discipline. The twins' fireworks revolt, where the school erupts in celebration of her humiliation, is the film's best joke because the audience has been made to want it that badly.

## What works

The compression. Rotten Tomatoes' critics consensus puts the feat plainly: it is not easy to take the longest Harry Potter book and streamline it into the shortest HP film, and Yates does the job. The film runs 138 minutes against the book's 38 chapters of Hogwarts bureaucratic warfare, and it keeps the political spine while cutting the house-elf subplot entirely.

The climax. The Department of Mysteries is the franchise's best staging of a real battle: the prophecy shelf room, Death Eaters smashing through the giant clock face, the veil, and the Dumbledore-Voldemort duel in the Atrium that finally shows the two most powerful wizards in the world fighting at full strength. Nicholas Hooper's score does its best work there, a striding piece under the duel that Time Out's review matched to "some thrilling set-pieces."

The new faces. Evanna Lynch's Luna arrives fully formed, and her scenes with the thestrals give the film its melancholy. Helena Bonham Carter's Bellatrix debuts as the Death Eaters' feral aristocrat. The New Yorker's Bruce Diones noted a leaner, slightly meaner Daniel Radcliffe carrying the anger the plot requires.

## What doesn't

Grawp. The giant is the film's one wholesale visual failure: a effects-heavy stretch of the plot that stalls the third act for a sequence of shadowy animation and screeching, and the film could lose him without losing anything else. The cramping also costs the supporting cast. Gary Oldman's Sirius, the emotional stakes of the whole Ministry operation, gets a handful of scenes, and the book's B-level cast (Kreacher, the Quibbler's full run, St. Mungo's) exists here only as names.

The look divides too. The film's blue-grey digital palette suits the surveillance state Yates is shooting, and it makes the franchise look less like a storybook than any entry before it. Kirk Honeycutt's Hollywood Reporter review called the film "full of plot but little fun," and David Ansen's Newsweek review scored it 50 with the complaint that "for all the special-effects wizardry, that touch of film magic never surfaces." Both land against the middle hour, where the film stacks council meetings.

## Order of the Phoenix ending explained

Spoiler-free summary first. The student army gets its test in the Ministry's depths, the prophecy everyone wants is destroyed, and a death in the family sends the war from schoolyard to open conflict. The film closes at the school lake with the five friends walking out together, the secret army now public knowledge.

**Spoiler warning. The rest of this section explains the ending in full.**

The prophecy, once retrieved, is a flat shard of glass and a bigger problem than Voldemort: it says a boy born at the end of July will have the power to defeat him, and it could have named Harry or Neville. The Death Eaters ambush the students in the Hall of Prophecies, the shelves collapse in the film's best sound design, and the Order arrives to hold the room. Bellatrix kills Sirius, who falls backwards through the veil and does not come back. The duel that follows is the first time Dumbledore and Voldemort fight on screen, and it ends when Voldemort possesses Harry, who throws him out with the thought of his friends. Fudge sees Voldemort with his own eyes, which ends the denial campaign in a single shot.

The meaning lands in the last scene. Harry tells his friends that they have something Voldemort does not have, that they have each other, and the film closes on the five walking to the courtyard with the war now officially underway. The rebellion the Ministry feared at the start of the film has become the resistance the saga runs on.

## The verdict

Is Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix worth watching? Yes, for Staunton alone, and for a climax that finally stages the saga's war as war. Yates turned the series' most interior book into its most political film, and the choice aged well: the surveillance, the propaganda, the school principal as functionary, all of it plays sharper in a post-2016 rewatch than the effects scenes of the same era. The deductions are real: Grawp, a supporting cast trimmed to headlines, and a grey wash that makes the film's beauty only ever come from performance. Metacritic's 71 marks the series' first real critic split, and the 81 percent audience score on the Popcornmeter marks the fans' disagreement with it.
