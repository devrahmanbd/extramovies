Spider-Man: Brand New Day (2026)
Dir. Destin Daniel Cretton | 145 min | PG-13 | US digital release October 6, 2026

Worth watching, with one warning about the length. This is a bruised, quiet film about a man nobody knows, and the sadness in it arrives late. It also runs long, and the climax is over before the characters are.

The setup does the heavy work. Peter Parker erased himself from the world's memory at the end of No Way Home to save it. Four years on he lives alone in a rusted New York attic with a wall of screens and no friends. MJ and Ned are at MIT and do not know him. He works full-time as Spider-Man, which now means small jobs, a mask, and going home to nobody. Then a body-hopping psychic outbreak starts eating the city and the Department of Damage Control contracts Spider-Man to stop it. Peter thinks the threat is a mind controller, and the body it lands in most often belongs to a grandmother who looks a great deal like Aunt May.

The middle of the film is Peter following MJ around New York, watching her with a new boyfriend, and not saying anything. That is the real subject, and it is an unusual thing for a Marvel picture to spend an act on.

Tom Holland is 30 and playing a man rather than a boy, and it reads in the shoulders. He stands differently in the mask and the tiredness in the voice is real rather than performed. The Guardian's Peter Bradshaw caught it as Holland being "back as the vexed webslinger in a sometimes shocking, occasionally sluggish new instalment."

Zendaya gets the best material. MJ is a civilian with a career and a boyfriend and no idea that the man she is talking to in a coffee shop saved the world twice. Bradshaw's verdict is flat: "Zendaya saves this film. She rescues it from silliness and the generic deja vu overfamiliarity that is always threatening to sink it." Sadie Sink plays Jean Grey and is the most interesting new thing in the MCU, an X-Man with a rewritten origin and a reason for what she does. Jon Bernthal's Punisher is a small, abrasive performance as the one other person in New York who understands being alone with a secret.

What it does not do is earn its length. The Los Angeles Times named the cause: the screenwriters add too much padding with their need to include Hulk, the new Black Widow and a radicalised Department of Damage Control, all of it in place before Avengers: Doomsday. Gleiberman at Variety reached the same verdict from the other side and called the film "arduous, overly episodic, and half an hour too long." His criticism of the romance is the sharpest one in the file: "We want the relationship between Peter and MJ to be a journey, not a frozen piece of sad irony."

There is real craft underneath. The isolation is the best idea in the picture, and Amy Nicholson at the Los Angeles Times named the franchise problem in her headline, with the film "finds its nimble hero tangled by franchise upkeep." The body horror is played as a burden rather than a power-up, with Peter unable to switch his new powers off. Bradshaw called it "the David Cronenberg zone."

The numbers. Rotten Tomatoes has it at 90 percent from 439 critic reviews, with a 97 percent Popcornmeter from more than 25,000 verified ratings. Metacritic has a Metascore of 66 from 56 critic reviews, labelled Generally Favorable, plus a user score of 7.9 from 1,120 user ratings. IMDb has it at 7.9 out of 10 from 360,000 ratings. Letterboxd members have it at a weighted 4.03 from 4,580,007 ratings, with 2,006,580 written reviews. The 66 is the lowest score any aggregator gave it, and the audience figures point the other way.

In the US there is no subscription service yet. It is for rent and for purchase on Amazon Video, the Apple TV Store and Fandango At Home, and for rental on Google Play Movies and YouTube. The digital release was October 6, 2026 and the disc is dated December 15, 2026.

It is the most grown-up film Marvel has made, and it is carried by two actors rather than by the plot. Gleiberman's summary was that the film is "more busy and self-serious than it is emotionally involving." He was close. The picture is busy. The emotion is real, and it arrives about a reel too late.
