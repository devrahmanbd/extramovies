The Odyssey (2026)
Dir. Christopher Nolan | 173 min | R | US release July 2026

Yes, and this is the rare blockbuster that earns the three hours it asks for. Christopher Nolan's Greek epic is vast and strangely cold, and both of those are facts about the film rather than about your appetite on the day.

Start with the format, because it changes everything. This is the first feature film shot entirely on IMAX film, and the first feature-length commercial film released entirely in 70mm IMAX. The difference shows in the way a body feels heavy. The ships have weight. The horses strain. When a wall comes down you feel the heat of it. The Guardian's Peter Bradshaw described "gasp-inducing, Imax-sized landscapes of loneliness shot by cinematographer Hoyte van Hoytema", and noted that he "avoids the sea's traditional cliched colour".

The story is not the monsters. Odysseus has been away from Ithaca for twenty years, and the island has been ruled by nobody since he left for Troy. Penelope holds the house together while the suitors eat her food and drink her wine. Their son Telemachus goes looking for his father. The film opens in a banquet hall with a man standing on a table telling the story of the Trojan Horse, and then it starts rearranging time. The structure is a deck of cards: distant past, recent past and present, shuffled.

Matt Damon plays Odysseus with a face that has been worn down to a mask, and Bradshaw noticed the technique exactly: "his boyish, almost cherubic face turned into a careworn mask of sadness." He is a trickster who keeps breaking his own code of honour, and the film plays that as a character flaw rather than as a wink. Anne Hathaway's Penelope is the surprise, furious and running the room while the men in it drink her wine. Charlize Theron's Calypso is the other surprise, a woman who genuinely wants him to stay. Robert Pattinson plays Antinous as a smooth and sneering piece of work. Lupita Nyong'o doubles as Helen and Clytemnestra. Travis Scott plays the storyteller who opens the film and cowrote the closing credits music.

The honesty about war is what the film gets right. The sacking of Troy is not a triumph scene. Matt Zoller Seitz at RogerEbert.com used Penelope's anger at the patriarchal entitlement that lets the suitors strip Ithaca of its riches as his first example of the production serving the characters' feelings. Odysseus's guilt is the engine of the plot. He was not delayed by the gods. He was delayed by the war and by his refusal to look at what the war did.

Where it falls short is the emotional temperature. Guy Lodge at Variety headed his review "Christopher Nolan's Vast, Thrilling, Slightly Aloof Epic". His summary was that the film "thrills generously for the bulk of its near three-hour running time", but "it's never exactly moving". The picture keeps your eyes busy and your heart somewhere else. The middle has a lot of doors in it, and the film opens several it does not need. The dialogue has broad-brush moments, and Matt Zoller Seitz answered the complaint about the modern register with one line: "One might as well complain that the actors aren't all speaking Greek."

On the numbers. Rotten Tomatoes has it at 94 percent from 515 critic reviews, with a 96 percent Popcornmeter from more than 25,000 verified ratings. Metacritic has a Metascore of 88 from 63 critic reviews, labelled Universal Acclaim, plus a user score of 5.7 from 3,346 user ratings. IMDb has it at 8.4 out of 10 from 534,000 ratings. Letterboxd members have it at a weighted 4.34 from 4,611,722 ratings, with 2,052,076 written reviews.

That 5.7 is the number to sit with. It is the only audience measure that dissents, and 3,346 ratings is enough to notice. Some of it is the 173-minute running time, and some of it is that the film rewards patience instead of forgiving it.

It is still in American cinemas, and at the time of writing there was no streaming or rental offer for it in the US. Universal lists it for digital on November 17, 2026, with 4K Ultra HD, Blu-ray and DVD on the same day, and rental on Apple TV and Prime Video. No subscription date has been announced.

This is a war film about the end of war, made with the best tools the industry has, and it earns the three hours it asks for. Lodge's objection is the right one and it is a small one: the film is a little too pleased with itself, a little too cool to let you in. Nolan's most personal picture since Oppenheimer is also his least consoling, which is a defensible choice for a story about a man who cannot go home.
