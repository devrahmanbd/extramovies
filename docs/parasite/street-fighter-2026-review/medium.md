## Title

Street Fighter review: the moves are right, the plot is a mess

## Subtitle

Kitao Sakurai's game adaptation gets the fights exactly right and then spends two hours on four storylines at once.

---

## Copy to paste

Yes, Street Fighter is worth watching for the fights, and for not much else. Sakurai's adaptation of the Capcom games hands you 120 minutes of game-accurate moves, fully animated interludes, and a cast of people who plainly grew up on the arcade cabinet. The story is where it falls apart. Four plotlines share the same running time, most of the tournament happens off screen, and the screenplay never finds a shape for the thing it is about.

Critically and commercially, this is the interesting case in the batch. Rotten Tomatoes has it at 42 percent from 120 critic reviews, with a Popcornmeter at 79 percent from more than 1,000 verified ratings. That is a thirty-seven point gap between the critics and the people who actually paid to go in.

## The fight work is the thing

Cinematographer Ken Seng shoots the film through artificial grain and 1980s neon, and the choreography uses the game's special moves with vocal callouts, so a Hadouken reads as a Hadouken. The fully animated sequences are the part worth seeing. The A.V. Club's Jacob Oller called them "by far the best-looking, best-sounding, most coherent moments of the movie", and the reason holds up, because the live action keeps dumping the emotion onto them.

Helen O'Hara at Empire thought the joke was on the moviegoer. After the failed 1994 adaptation and the recent Mortal Kombat films, her verdict was that "the sucker-punch is that it's actually quite fun". The film knows a fight game is a comedy with rules, and it plays those rules straight for long enough to land.

## The story that never arrives

The set-up starts in 1987, in a fully animated anime prologue, at the first World Warrior tournament. Ryu beats Sagat with the Dark Hadou, the demon Akuma notices, and Ryu is left with two problems: his master Gouken, whose death comes soon after the fight, and an adoptive brother, Ken Masters, who blames him for the rift. Ryu disappears into a life of handing out ramen on the street. Ken becomes a celebrity, a show fighter doing commercials for colas and batteries.

Six years later, in 1993, M. Bison stages the second World Warrior tournament in Metrolao City. Chun-Li, an undercover Interpol agent working on her father's murder, recruits both men. Ryu only enters because Akuma has threatened to kill whoever wins, and he wants Ken out of the bracket. Bison's real plan is a device that pulls the fighting power out of a champion, which is what Chun-Li is trying to stop. By the middle of the picture, Ryu's own story has been pushed to the side.

There is too much here for two hours. Ryu is jailed for a large stretch of the runtime, which leaves Andrew Koji stranded as the film's earnest face in a movie full of loud ones, and the tournament the whole story is built around keeps getting skipped. Oller called the result a film that "merely pratfalls for two excruciating hours of bad jokes, worse combat, and a plot that crumbles under the weight of its character select screen". RogerEbert.com's Clint Worthington put it another way: "the punishing two-hour runtime ends in a flagrant call for a sequel, making the whole affair feel even more unfinished than it already was".

The cut is relentless. The film moves between Ken and Ryu's estrangement, Chun-Li's revenge plot, Bison's machine and Dan's insecurity at a speed that leaves nothing landing, and the jokes get undercut by the next cut. O'Hara, the kindest critic in the file, still found "far too much plot", a "nearly unforgivable sequel-baiting ending", and a 4 Non Blondes song playing over the 1987 scenes, since the band did not release it until 1993.

## The numbers, and where to watch

Rotten Tomatoes has the Tomatometer at 42 percent from 120 reviews and the Popcornmeter at 79 percent from more than 1,000 verified ratings. The platform's critics consensus says the film "has plenty of affection for its source material and doesn't lack for dizzying style, but its incoherent storytelling and juvenile humor make for a disappointing regression among video game adaptations". Metacritic has a user score of 6.8 from 174 ratings. Letterboxd members have it at a member average of 2.81 from 103,183 ratings, with 55,024 written reviews.

At the time of writing it was still in US cinemas, with no subscription service for it in the United States. The way in was a purchase, with the digital release on Fandango At Home, and the disc from Amazon DVD / Blu-ray. It opened on October 16, 2026.

## The short version

Take the fights, leave the plot. If you are a fighting game fan and you can sit through a story that never quite arrives, it is worth your evening. If you need the story to hold together, it is not.
