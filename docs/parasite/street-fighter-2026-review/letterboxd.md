Street Fighter (2026)
Dir. Kitao Sakurai | 120 min | PG-13 | US release October 16, 2026

Worth watching for the fights, and for not much else. Sakurai's adaptation of the Capcom games hands you 120 minutes of game-accurate moves, fully animated interludes, and a cast of people who plainly grew up on the arcade cabinet. The story is where it falls apart. Four plotlines share the same running time, most of the tournament happens off screen, and the screenplay never finds a shape for the thing it is about.

The set-up starts in 1987, in a fully animated anime prologue, at the first World Warrior tournament. Ryu beats Sagat with the Dark Hadou, the demon Akuma notices, and Ryu is left with two problems: his master Gouken, whose death comes soon after the fight, and an adoptive brother, Ken Masters, who blames him for the rift. Ryu disappears into a life of handing out ramen on the street. Ken becomes a celebrity, a show fighter doing commercials for colas and batteries.

Six years later, in 1993, the dictator M. Bison stages the second World Warrior tournament in Metrolao City. Chun-Li, an undercover Interpol agent working on her father's murder, recruits both men. Ryu only enters because Akuma has threatened to kill whoever wins, and he wants Ken out of the bracket. Bison's real plan is a device that pulls the fighting power out of a champion, which is what Chun-Li is trying to stop. By the middle of the picture, Ryu's own story, the one about the tournament and the demon, has been pushed to the side.

The fights, and the look of them, are the reason to go. Cinematographer Ken Seng shoots through artificial grain and 1980s neon, and the choreography uses the game's special moves with vocal callouts. The fully animated sequences are the part worth seeing. The A.V. Club's Jacob Oller called them "by far the best-looking, best-sounding, most coherent moments of the movie", and the reason holds up, because the live action keeps dumping the emotion on them.

Noah Centineo plays Ken as a golden boy who has been coasting on his name, and he lands it. RogerEbert.com's Clint Worthington found a "Kurt Russell in Big Trouble in Little China" quality in the himbo register. Callina Liang's Chun-Li is the best thing in the film, a burnt-out Interpol agent who lies well and does not have time for the other fighters' jokes. David Dastmalchian plays M. Bison with a curled sneer, and Jason Momoa turns up as Blanka in what is close to a cameo, a green monster with a few surprises in the performance. Cody Rhodes gives Guile an absurdly tall flat top, Orville Peck wears a mask as Vega because his stage persona already wears one, and 50 Cent plays the boxer Balrog, which is a joke that pays off in the ring. Joe Anoa'i, better known as Roman Reigns, plays Akuma.

What does not work is the script, which has too many fighters and not enough room. Ryu is jailed for a large stretch of the runtime, which leaves Andrew Koji stranded as the film's earnest face in a movie full of loud ones, and the tournament keeps getting skipped. Oller called the result a film that "merely pratfalls for two excruciating hours of bad jokes, worse combat, and a plot that crumbles under the weight of its character select screen". Worthington put it another way: "the punishing two-hour runtime ends in a flagrant call for a sequel, making the whole affair feel even more unfinished than it already was."

The cut is relentless. The film moves between Ken and Ryu's estrangement, Chun-Li's revenge plot, Bison's machine and Dan's insecurity at a speed that leaves nothing landing, and the jokes get undercut by the next cut. Helen O'Hara at Empire was the kindest of the critics on record and still found "far too much plot", a "nearly unforgivable sequel-baiting ending", and a 4 Non Blondes song playing over the 1987 scenes, since the band did not release it until 1993.

The split is the story here. Rotten Tomatoes has the Tomatometer at 42 percent from 120 reviews and the Popcornmeter at 79 percent from more than 1,000 verified ratings, a thirty-seven point gap between the two. The platform's critics consensus says the film "has plenty of affection for its source material and doesn't lack for dizzying style, but its incoherent storytelling and juvenile humor make for a disappointing regression among video game adaptations". Metacritic's user score is 6.8 from 174 ratings, and Letterboxd is the outlier on the other side, with a member average of 2.81 from 103,183 ratings and 55,024 written reviews.

At the time of writing it was still in US cinemas, with no subscription service for it in the United States. The way in was a purchase, with the digital release on Fandango At Home, and the disc from Amazon DVD / Blu-ray.

Take the fights, leave the plot. If you are a fighting game fan and you can sit through a story that never quite arrives, it is worth your evening. If you need the story to hold together, it is not.
